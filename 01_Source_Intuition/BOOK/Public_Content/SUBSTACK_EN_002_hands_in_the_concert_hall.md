# The Hands in the Concert Hall

> Scene · on where the roads you didn't take actually go

You studied engineering. The year you chose your major, you spent a long time torn between engineering and music. You picked engineering — and you can't quite reconstruct why anymore. Maybe your parents' hopes, maybe the job prospects, maybe you just didn't have the nerve that summer. Either way you chose, and then you walked for fifteen years.

Now you're sitting in a concert hall. A friend gave you the ticket. A piano recital. The lights dim. The music starts.

At first you're just listening. But a few minutes in, you notice something: your fingers are moving. Not tapping along — something subtler. A faint tingle in the fingertips, as if they remember a touch they never quite learned. Your right hand curls a few times against your thigh, reaching for something.

You can't name it. It isn't regret — regret is a feeling you can put into the sentence "if only I had." This is deeper and less clear: a path was hinted at in your body and never walked to the end, and fifteen years later it's still there. Not as a memory. As an unfinished possibility your fingers know but your language can't reach.

## The road not taken didn't disappear

Here is the thing we usually get wrong. We treat the choices we didn't make as simply *gone* — options that left the stage, deleted from reality. You chose A; B and C are just the ones you didn't pick.

But B doesn't vanish. It becomes the *backside* of the shape you actually are.

You are this shape — engineer, this city, these friends, this life. But that shape has an outline you can't usually feel, drawn by the music you didn't choose fifteen years ago. The outline isn't on your résumé, isn't in the story you tell about yourself. It's in your fingers, in the small tightening in your chest when a certain melody plays.

## What gets left out keeps working

Every time something takes form, it pushes other forms aside. And what gets pushed aside doesn't stop existing — it keeps acting, in three quiet ways.

It can become a **shadow** — an unbidden reference point that recalibrates how you feel about the road you're on. The person who seriously considered emigrating and stayed experiences "here" differently from the person who never considered it, even doing the identical job in the identical city.

It can become **tension** — stress written into a structure. A river that cut left left the right bank untouched and steep; the unchosen direction is written into the geology.

It can become **friction** — the rising cost of going back. At forty, learning piano costs far more than it did at eighteen, not because the road moved, but because you walked so far down another one.

## The question under the scene

Read your own shape, and you'll find it records two things at once: what you repeatedly chose, and what you repeatedly turned away from. A canyon's shape records both the water's path and the banks the water never crossed.

So here is the question this opens — the one the chapter it comes from spends itself on:

**The possibilities you didn't choose — are they really gone? Or do they go on shaping you, from the back of the shape you became?**
