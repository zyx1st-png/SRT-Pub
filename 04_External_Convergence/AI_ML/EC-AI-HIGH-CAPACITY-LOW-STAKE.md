# Evidence Card: AI / High Capacity Low Stake as a Candidate Interface for `d-value`, Consequence Return, Selection Ownership, and Subjecthood Threshold

This draft card records a sourced structural / conceptual contrast candidate. It is not canonical, not accepted evidence, and not proof of SRT.

## 1. External finding

Recent AI systems can show high inference capacity, fluent language behavior, tool use, planning-like behavior, and increasingly agentic task performance. At the same time, the checked literature separates these capabilities from several stronger conditions:

- RLHF and preference modeling train models toward human-preferred outputs or reward-model objectives; they do not by themselves establish stake-coupled concern.
- Agentic-system evaluations measure task completion, autonomy, or long-horizon capability; they do not by themselves establish consequence-bearing subjecthood.
- LLM self-reports and self-assessments are limited evidence for internal state, subjecthood, or consciousness.
- AI consciousness / welfare assessment work generally treats consciousness as an open assessment problem using theory-based indicators, not as a conclusion from fluency alone.
- Persistent memory, embodiment, self-maintenance, and consequence-bearing loops are live architectural pressure points for future classification.

For SRT, this makes current AI a candidate structural contrast: high capacity can appear without clearly demonstrated `d-value`, consequence return, or subjecthood threshold. That contrast is not a universal conclusion about all current or future AI systems.

### 1.1 Prakash–Friston focused convergence: error must return to a continuing bearer

Ayush Prakash and Karl Friston's July 2026 essay, “The Infinity of the Brain and the Void of the Machine,” offers a philosophically concentrated version of this contrast. Its strongest claim is not merely that biological brains predict or update, but that error and correction return to the same continuing, embodied agent and alter what that agent can subsequently do. The essay contrasts this with dominant LLM-style deployments, in which the downstream cost of hallucination or failure is usually borne by users, institutions, operators, or affected third parties rather than clearly returning to the model as its own non-substitutable future burden.

The essay therefore supplies focused external convergence for four separations:

- prediction capacity is not yet consequence-bearing agency;
- behavioral correction is not yet ownership of the correction history;
- externally imposed retraining is not automatically self-revision by the same bearer;
- a system's output can have major consequences without those consequences becoming stakes for that system's own continuity.

For this card, **selection ownership** is used only as a provisional diagnostic label: whether a selection's consequence-bearing history is bound to the same continuing bearer that generated the selection and whether that history changes the bearer's future reachable states, resources, policies, or repair obligations. This label does not create a new canonical variable, metric, or subjecthood criterion.

The essay also makes stronger claims — for example, that LLMs have no world model, never meaningfully update, are “just code,” or can never cross the relevant boundary. Those claims are treated here as rhetorically overbroad and are not adopted by SRT. The useful convergence is the consequence-return structure, not a material or permanent biological / artificial divide.

## 5. Evidence level: E0-E5

E2 = structural convergence / conceptual contrast.

Reason for E2: The checked AI literature motivates a careful separation between performance capacity, reward optimization, preference following, agentic task behavior, and stronger claims about internal stake or subjecthood. The Prakash–Friston essay adds a focused philosophical formulation: a mistake matters differently when its consequences return to and alter the same continuing bearer. That separation is compatible with SRT's distinction between capacity-like proxies and `d-value` as stake-coupled concern.

Reason not to rate E3: this draft card does not provide a validated operational proxy for `d-value`, consequence return, selection ownership, or subjecthood threshold in AI systems. The Prakash–Friston source is a philosophical essay, not an empirical validation study, and several of its class-level claims about LLMs are stronger than the evidence warrants.

## 6. Why it matters

SRT treats `d-value` as stake-coupled concern, not merely bandwidth, fluency, reward sensitivity, or task competence. AI systems are therefore a useful test case for separating capacity from concern.

Current dominant LLM-style deployments often present high inference capacity without clear evidence of direct stake-coupled consequence return to the model itself, but future architectures with persistent memory, embodiment, self-maintenance, or consequence-bearing loops may require revised classification.

The relevance is structural:

- RLHF and preference modeling can generate helpful or aligned behavior without showing that consequences return to the system as non-substitutable stake.
- Agentic task evaluations can show tool use, planning, and long-horizon competence without deciding whether the agent bears the cost of its outcomes.
- Self-reports may be fluent and context-sensitive without being reliable evidence of subjecthood.
- AI consciousness assessment work suggests that architecture and functional indicators matter more than ordinary language fluency alone.
- Persistent-agent and embodied-agent research shows why the boundary must remain open rather than frozen around present LLM deployments.
- Active-inference framing sharpens the question from “does the system predict?” to “does uncertainty, error, and correction reorganize the same continuing bearer?”

### 6.1 Bearer-first diagnostic

Before assigning stake, agency, or subjecthood, the candidate bearer must be specified. In AI systems, at least five units can be confused:

1. the base model weights;
2. one inference process or session;
3. a persistent agent composed of model, memory, tools, and policies;
4. the deployed technical service;
5. the institution or human–machine organization operating the service.

A consequence that returns to one unit cannot automatically be attributed to another. For example, a company paying a fine, an engineer updating a model, or a user correcting a response does not by itself show that the original model instance owns the consequence history.

A scoped selection-ownership diagnostic should therefore ask:

- Which exact bearer generated the selection?
- Is the relevant history retained by that same bearer across time?
- Does failure alter that bearer's future reachable options, resources, policies, or continuity?
- Must repair be undertaken by the same bearer rather than an external operator or replacement copy?
- Can reset, replication, rollback, or substitution erase the burden at negligible cost to the candidate bearer?
- Are the consequences merely represented, or do they constrain the bearer's own continued organization?

These questions do not by themselves establish consciousness or responsibility. They identify whether “the system learned from its mistake” refers to bearer-bound consequence return or only to external system maintenance.

This makes AI / high capacity low stake a candidate interface for clarifying `d-value`, consequence return, selection ownership, and subjecthood threshold. It does not settle the status of current or future systems.

## 7. Alternative explanations

The same external findings can be explained without SRT:

- Functionalist accounts may argue that sufficiently organized behavior or information processing is already enough for relevant mental status.
- Reward-centered accounts may treat RL reward, reward modeling, or preference satisfaction as a sufficient proxy for concern.
- Behaviorist evaluations may treat fluent language and task performance as enough for practical attribution.
- Tool-use and agent-benchmark failures may reflect engineering immaturity rather than absence of stake.
- Deployment context may create external stakes for users, firms, or institutions even if those stakes do not return to the model itself.
- Future AI systems with persistent identity, self-maintenance, embodiment, resource constraints, or stable memory may satisfy stronger conditions than present systems.
- A functionalist may reject non-substitutability and bearer continuity as necessary, arguing that the relevant organization can survive copying, migration, or distributed implementation.
- An active-inference implementation may exhibit uncertainty-sensitive exploration and self-correction without thereby establishing phenomenal consciousness or normative responsibility.

The Prakash–Friston essay's biological / artificial contrast can also be explained as a contingent difference between current architectures rather than a principled material boundary.

## 8. What would weaken this

This card should be downgraded toward E1 if:

- high-capacity AI behavior cannot be cleanly separated from architecture-level consequence return;
- a future system shows persistent self-maintenance, non-substitutable future burden, and outcome loops that return to its own continuity;
- reward / preference-model objectives become directly tied to system-level self-preservation, repair, or irreversible loss;
- self-report methods become independently reliable indicators of internal state rather than ordinary language behavior;
- the card is used to make a universal class conclusion about AI subjecthood rather than a scoped contrast;
- bearer identity cannot be specified consistently across copying, rollback, model updates, tool changes, and distributed execution;
- the proposed selection-ownership diagnostic adds no discrimination beyond ordinary persistence, memory, or reinforcement-learning measures.

It should be routed to a pressure ledger if future architectures make the high-capacity / low-stake contrast too coarse.

## 9. Boundary: what this does not prove

This card does not establish any canonical SRT definition.

Specifically:

- High capability does not by itself establish `d-value`.
- Fluent language behavior does not by itself establish subjecthood.
- RL reward, preference modeling, or instruction following are not automatically stake-coupled concern.
- Current AI systems should not be classified as universally lacking or possessing subjecthood as a class conclusion.
- This card is a structural / conceptual differential-support candidate, not proof of SRT.
- It does not treat present LLM deployments as the final form of AI agency.
- It does not deny that future systems with persistent memory, embodiment, self-maintenance, or consequence-bearing loops may require revised classification.
- Active inference, prediction-error minimization, curiosity-like exploration, or online updating do not by themselves establish consciousness, subjecthood, or responsibility.
- Representational world-model evidence does not by itself establish stake ownership; conversely, the absence of a human-like world model does not prove the absence of every relevant form of agency.
- “Selection ownership” in this card is a provisional diagnostic phrase, not a canonical SRT construct or scalar.
- The Prakash–Friston essay is not evidence that LLM-based systems can never change, never build world representations, or never become components of a stronger agent architecture.
- No evidence card may override the canonical `d-value` file.

## 10. Upgrade path

Possible next steps:

1. Create a bridge note separating capacity, reward, preference following, agency, stake-coupling, consequence return, selection ownership, and subjecthood.
2. Define a scoped lab hypothesis for architecture-level consequence return: persistent memory, self-maintenance, embodiment, resource constraints, and non-substitutable future burden.
3. Build a bearer-disambiguation protocol that distinguishes model weights, runtime instance, persistent agent, deployed service, and institution before assigning stakes.
4. Test whether failure produces bearer-bound changes that cannot be reduced to external retraining, database updates, prompt changes, or cheap instance replacement.
5. Upgrade toward E3 only if operational criteria distinguish high capacity from stake-coupled concern better than simpler behavioral, persistence, memory, or functional accounts.
6. Add a pressure-ledger entry in a later pass for future AI systems that blur the high-capacity / low-stake boundary.
7. Keep public summaries cautious: this card is about a diagnostic contrast, not an AI sentience verdict or a biological-exclusivity claim.
