# SRT Ethics Casebook

> **Purpose**: Turn SRT ethics guardrails into usable case diagnostics.  
> **Status**: Applied ethics bridge / casebook. It does not define canonical moral primitives.  
> **Use rule**: Use this file to analyze concrete ethical cases without collapsing moral reality, moral intensity, and moral legitimacy.

---

## 0. Core distinction

SRT ethics should keep three things separate:

```text
moral reality      = a norm / value is socially or personally real as L2 constraint;
moral intensity    = how much d-value / concern-weight it carries;
moral legitimacy   = whether it preserves future selectability, avoids friction export, and allows correction / repair.
```

Guardrail:

```text
real norm != intense norm != legitimate norm
```

A cruel norm can be real.  
A fanatical norm can be intense.  
A stable norm can still be illegitimate.

## 1. Standard ethics case template

Use this template for concrete cases.

```md
## Case: <Name>

### 1. Case description

### 2. Relevant L2

What stabilized norms, roles, rules, habits, institutions, or expectations structure the case?

### 3. Affected subjects

Who has d-value-bearing future selectability at stake?

### 4. Moral intensity

What is strongly cared about? By whom? Is the intensity justified or distorted?

### 5. Moral reality

What norms are actually operative, whether or not they are legitimate?

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Does the structure pass:

- future-selectability test;
- friction-export test;
- correction / appeal test;
- subjecthood-protection test;
- reversibility / repair test?

### 7. Hidden Psi_f

Who pays invisible cost, emotional burden, compliance labor, shame, risk, or transition friction?

### 8. Future selectability

Whose future options expand? Whose future options narrow?

### 9. Responsibility / culpability / repair

Use:

```text
moral weight      ~ affected future-selectability
responsibility    ~ accessible meta-selection capacity
culpability       ~ responsibility - structural blockage - unpayable friction
repair obligation ~ capacity to reduce future harm / restore selectability
```

### 10. What would improve the structure?

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

What would show that SRT adds little beyond ordinary ethics or institutional analysis?
```

## 2. Case 1 — Family obligation

### 1. Case description

A family member is expected to provide unpaid care, financial help, emotional labor, or obedience because “this is what family means.”

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
family role norms;
filial duty;
gendered care expectations;
intergenerational obligation;
shame / gratitude scripts.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- caregiver;
- care receiver;
- other family members;
- absent or hidden beneficiaries;
- future children / dependents if care burden cascades.

### 4. Moral intensity

Family obligations often carry high `d-value` because they affect identity, belonging, gratitude, and abandonment risk.

### 5. Moral reality

The obligation can be real as `L2`: it constrains choices through shame, expectation, love, dependency, and reputation.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

A family obligation is more legitimate when:

```text
care burden is shareable;
exit / negotiation is possible;
the caregiver's future selectability is preserved;
care receiver's dignity is protected;
repair and reciprocity channels exist.
```

It is less legitimate when:

```text
one person pays hidden Psi_f;
care is coerced by shame;
structural alternatives are blocked;
refusal destroys belonging;
the caregiver's life trajectory collapses.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

Often paid as time, lost opportunities, emotional exhaustion, guilt, health cost, career interruption.

### 8. Future selectability

SRT asks:

```text
Does the obligation preserve more futures than it destroys?
```

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Responsibility should be distributed across family roles, institutions, welfare systems, and those with capacity to relieve burden.

### 10. Improvement

- explicit burden sharing;
- care schedules;
- compensation or recognition;
- respite care;
- negotiation without shame;
- preserving caregiver future selectability.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If ordinary duty / care ethics explains all relevant burden and legitimacy patterns, SRT's added value should be narrowed to friction-export and future-selectability analysis.

## 3. Case 2 — School discipline

### 1. Case description

A student violates a school rule and is punished to preserve order.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
classroom order;
authority hierarchy;
obedience norms;
achievement ranking;
behavioral compliance system.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- student punished;
- classmates;
- teacher;
- parents;
- future student identity.

### 4. Moral intensity

Schools often treat order as high `d-value`, but the student's future self-concept may also carry high `d-value`.

### 5. Moral reality

The rule is real if it shapes behavior, punishment, expectation, and reputation.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Legitimacy depends on whether discipline:

```text
restores future selectability;
teaches re-selection;
protects others;
avoids humiliation;
provides appeal / explanation;
distinguishes mistake from identity condemnation.
```

Illegitimate discipline exports `Psi_f` by turning institutional order into shame, learned helplessness, or identity damage.

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

Shame, fear, status loss, mistrust, anxiety, future avoidance.

### 8. Future selectability

Good discipline expands future self-regulation. Bad discipline narrows identity into “bad student.”

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Student responsibility depends on age, alternatives, understanding, and capacity for re-selection. Teacher/institution responsibility includes proportionality and repair pathway.

### 10. Improvement

- restorative discipline;
- explicit reason-giving;
- behavior repair instead of identity punishment;
- appeal channel;
- support for re-selection.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If standard restorative justice explains the same pattern, SRT's contribution is the formal link between `L2` discipline, hidden `Psi_f`, and future selectability.

## 4. Case 3 — Medical triage

### 1. Case description

Medical staff prioritize patients under scarcity.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
triage protocols;
clinical priority rules;
resource allocation norms;
professional responsibility;
legal liability.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- patients;
- families;
- clinicians;
- institution;
- excluded or delayed patients.

### 4. Moral intensity

Life, death, dignity, pain, and family futures carry extreme `d-value`.

### 5. Moral reality

Protocols are real as `L2` because they structure who receives care first.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Legitimate triage must check:

```text
transparent criteria;
clinical relevance;
non-discrimination;
appeal / review where possible;
documentation;
burden sharing;
clinician moral injury support.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

Hidden cost may be paid by families, low-status patients, overworked clinicians, or those excluded by opaque criteria.

### 8. Future selectability

Triage should preserve the greatest possible future selectability without erasing individual dignity.

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Individual clinicians should not absorb all culpability when scarcity and protocol constraints are institutional. Repair includes review, explanation, compensation, and system redesign.

### 10. Improvement

- transparent criteria;
- documentation;
- second review;
- moral injury support;
- post-event audit;
- resource expansion.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If existing medical ethics fully covers the case, SRT's added value is mainly the hidden-friction and future-selectability vocabulary.

## 5. Case 4 — Eldercare responsibility

### 1. Case description

A family, institution, or community must decide how to care for an elderly or disabled person.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
filial norms;
institutional care routines;
medical bureaucracy;
insurance / subsidy rules;
community expectations;
family property / inheritance expectations.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- elder / disabled person;
- family caregivers;
- institution staff;
- funders;
- community service providers.

### 4. Moral intensity

Care carries high `d-value` because it affects dignity, dependency, bodily continuity, memory, and belonging.

### 5. Moral reality

Care arrangements become real `L2` when schedules, payments, roles, and routines constrain daily life.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

A care system is legitimate when it:

```text
preserves dignity;
reduces hidden caregiver friction;
keeps correction channels open;
protects the elder's remaining future selectability;
does not convert vulnerability into institutional invisibility.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

May appear as caregiver burnout, staff overload, elder loneliness, paperwork burden, medical navigation cost, and emotional guilt.

### 8. Future selectability

SRT asks:

```text
Does the care structure preserve agency appropriate to the elder's condition?
Does it also preserve caregiver futures?
```

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Responsibility is distributed across family, institution, medical system, policy, and community support. Culpability should account for structural blockage and unpayable friction.

### 10. Improvement

- shared care plan;
- transparent responsibility map;
- respite support;
- elder preference recording;
- complaint / correction channel;
- caregiver support;
- institutional feedback loops.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If care ethics and welfare policy analysis fully explain outcomes, SRT's added value is a structured map of `L2` routines, hidden `Psi_f`, and future-selectability preservation.

## 6. Case 5 — Algorithmic recommendation

### 1. Case description

A recommendation system shapes what users see, buy, believe, or become.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
platform ranking rules;
engagement optimization;
attention habit loops;
creator incentives;
advertising metrics;
content moderation policies.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- users;
- creators;
- advertisers;
- platform;
- communities affected by information cascades.

### 4. Moral intensity

The system may appear low-stakes locally, but repeated recommendations harden attention, identity, desire, and social belief.

### 5. Moral reality

The algorithm is real `L2` if it constrains future visibility and choice.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Legitimacy depends on whether the system:

```text
protects future selectability;
avoids addictive closure;
keeps correction / appeal available;
exposes ranking logic where appropriate;
prevents hidden Psi_f export to users or creators;
avoids dehumanizing d-exclusion.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

User attention fatigue, creator anxiety, social polarization, misinformation correction burden, compulsive scrolling, reduced alternative path visibility.

### 8. Future selectability

SRT asks whether the recommendation system expands meaningful options or narrows users into hardened loops.

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Responsibility lies primarily with platform designers, deployers, policy owners, and incentive structures. Users have some responsibility, but their selectable space is engineered.

### 10. Improvement

- user controls;
- explanation / appeal;
- diversity injection;
- friction for harmful loops;
- wellbeing metrics;
- audit trails;
- creator protection.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If engagement metrics reliably align with user future selectability and low friction export, SRT's critique should be narrowed.

## 7. Case 6 — Public shaming

### 1. Case description

A person is publicly condemned online or offline for a moral violation.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
reputation norms;
public punishment;
group boundary enforcement;
platform amplification;
identity labeling;
virtue signaling.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- accused person;
- harmed party;
- audience;
- community;
- platform / institution.

### 4. Moral intensity

Public shaming often carries high `d-value` because it concerns status, belonging, dignity, and moral identity.

### 5. Moral reality

The shame is real if it alters reputation, employment, belonging, safety, or future options.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Public condemnation is more legitimate when:

```text
facts are reliable;
harm is real;
response is proportional;
repair is possible;
appeal / correction exists;
the target is not reduced to permanent identity condemnation.
```

It is less legitimate when it becomes:

```text
friction export;
status performance;
permanent social death;
fact-free amplification;
collective cruelty.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

Mental health cost, reputation destruction, fear culture, audience moral fatigue, bystander silence.

### 8. Future selectability

SRT asks whether shaming repairs harm and protects future selectability, or whether it narrows everyone into fear and rigidity.

### 9. Responsibility / repair

The accused may bear responsibility if agency and norm access were present. The crowd/platform also bears responsibility for proportionality, verification, and repair channels.

### 10. Improvement

- fact checking;
- proportional response;
- restorative path;
- time limits;
- appeal channel;
- distinction between action and identity;
- platform amplification control.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If standard discourse ethics or restorative justice fully explains the dynamics, SRT adds the future-selectability and friction-export analysis.

## 8. Case 7 — AI tool misuse

### 1. Case description

A person or organization uses an AI tool to produce harmful output or make harmful decisions.

### 2. Relevant L2

```text
AI deployment workflow;
automation trust;
institutional policy;
user incentive;
liability structure;
review / oversight process.
```

### 3. Affected subjects

- harmed individuals;
- user/operator;
- deploying institution;
- developer/platform;
- broader public.

### 4. Moral intensity

The case becomes high `d-value` when it affects dignity, livelihood, rights, access, reputation, health, or future opportunity.

### 5. Moral reality

The AI system is a real constraint if it shapes decisions, access, classification, or social consequences.

### 6. Moral legitimacy

Legitimacy requires:

```text
human/institutional answerability;
transparent oversight;
appeal / correction channel;
audit trail;
proportional deployment;
hidden Psi_f analysis;
no false transfer of responsibility onto the AI.
```

### 7. Hidden `Psi_f`

Users may pay with confusion, appeal burden, misclassification cost, reputational harm, lost access, or forced compliance with opaque systems.

### 8. Future selectability

SRT asks:

```text
Does AI deployment expand user options, or lock people into opaque selections they cannot challenge?
```

### 9. Responsibility / repair

Use the AI agency note:

```text
AI-A0 / AI-A1 / AI-A2 / AI-A3?
```

For current systems, responsibility usually routes to:

```text
developer;
deployer;
user;
institution;
regulator;
platform incentives.
```

### 10. Improvement

- auditability;
- human review;
- appeal path;
- logging;
- user explanation;
- deployment limits;
- harm repair protocol.

### 11. Failure / narrowing condition

If ordinary product liability and AI ethics frameworks fully explain responsibility allocation, SRT's added value is the friction-export / future-selectability / responsibility-network framing.

## 10. Minimal conclusion

SRT ethics becomes practical when it asks:

```text
What L2 is operating?
Who carries d-value?
Who pays hidden Psi_f?
Whose future selectability expands or narrows?
Can the structure be corrected, appealed, exited, or repaired?
```

The key ethical move is not to ask only:

```text
Was the norm real?
Was the concern intense?
Was the system stable?
```

but to ask:

```text
Was the stabilized structure legitimate, or did it merely export friction and narrow futures?
```
