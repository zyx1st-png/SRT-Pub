## 1. One-paragraph abstract

Selective Reality Theory introduces `d-value` as a measure of concern-weighted non-substitutability: the degree to which a content, object, relation, or possibility changes what a system can preserve, lose, refuse, or become. This paper develops `d-value` as an ontology of concern distinct from salience, arousal, reward, preference intensity, utility, and moral goodness. Whereas salience marks priority, reward marks reinforcement, and utility marks preference ordering, `d-value` marks existential stake: the impact of a selection on future selectability and self/world continuity. The paper argues that `d-value` helps explain why some low-salience events become life-defining, why some high-salience events do not sediment, why pain, grief, shame, trauma, commitment, and moral obligation reorganize agency, and why subjecthood cannot be reduced to information processing alone. It also offers a non-reductive validation strategy: `d-value` should predict memory persistence, action reorganization, identity impact, and resistance to substitution beyond salience, confidence, reward, or arousal. The result is a bridge between ontology, consciousness, ethics, and cognitive science.

## 2. Core thesis

`d-value` names the existential weight of a selection.

Compact thesis:

```text
d-value = concern-weighted non-substitutability with consequences for future selectability
```

Longer thesis:

> `d-value` is not how noticeable something is, not how much pleasure it produces, not how strongly it is preferred, and not whether it is morally good. It is the degree to which a selection matters for what a subject or system can continue to be, do, preserve, lose, or refuse to exchange.

---

## 3. Contribution claim

The paper contributes six claims:

1. **Conceptual distinction**: `d-value` is distinct from salience, arousal, reward, preference, utility, and moral goodness.
2. **Ontological claim**: concern is not an optional psychological decoration but a selection-weighting structure that changes manifestation and hardening.
3. **Cognitive claim**: high-`d-value` contents should reorganize memory, attention, action, and policy beyond salience alone.
4. **Consciousness claim**: subjecthood requires structured concern; information processing alone is insufficient.
5. **Ethical claim**: moral intensity depends partly on `d-value`, but moral legitimacy requires additional tests.
6. **Validation claim**: `d-value` can be tested through proxy signatures and failure conditions.

## 5. Section plan

### Section 1 — The problem: what matters is not always what is salient

Aim:

> Show that salience, reward, preference, and utility do not exhaust mattering.

Opening examples:

```text
a quiet sentence that changes a life;
a small loss that becomes grief;
a low-salience obligation that structures years;
a high-salience advertisement that leaves no trace;
a painful event whose meaning matters more than intensity;
a promise whose value is destroyed by substitution.
```

Core contrast:

```text
salience = what stands out;
reward = what reinforces;
preference = what is chosen or ranked;
utility = what maximizes a modeled value;
d-value = what changes future selectability / self-world continuity.
```

---

### Section 2 — Defining `d-value`

Aim:

> Give a disciplined definition and guardrails.

Working definition:

```text
d-value = concern-weighted non-substitutability under selection
```

Alternative expression:

```text
d(x) ~ |partial U / partial S|
```

Formula role:

> bridge model / operational proxy candidate, not final scalar reduction.

Core components:

```text
concern;
non-substitutability;
future-selectability impact;
identity / boundary relevance;
cost willing to bear;
loss / refusal structure;
long-term hardening potential.
```

Guardrail:

```text
High d-value does not imply moral goodness.
```

### Section 3 — Distinguishing `d-value` from nearby constructs

| Nearby construct | What it captures | Why it is not `d-value` |
|---|---|---|
| Salience | attention priority / noticeability | high salience may leave no existential trace |
| Arousal | physiological activation | arousal can be high without durable concern |
| Reward | reinforcement signal | reward can be substitutable or addictive without true concern |
| Preference | chosen option / ranking | preferences can be shallow, manipulated, or unstable |
| Utility | modeled value maximization | utility may miss non-substitutability and identity stakes |
| Confidence | subjective certainty | certainty can attach to low-d contents |
| Moral goodness | normative legitimacy | high concern can support domination or fanaticism |
| Meaning | interpreted significance | meaning may be one expression of d-value, not identical to it |

Aim:

> Translate the concept into testable packages.

Main validation hypothesis:

> High-`d-value` contents should predict memory persistence, action reorganization, identity impact, and future decision weighting beyond salience, arousal, confidence, reward, or preference intensity.

Proxy families:

```text

willingness to bear cost;

```

Baseline:

```text

```

Failure condition:

```text
If salience, arousal, confidence, reward, and preference fully explain downstream effects,
```

### Section 9 — Objections and replies

Include at least these objections:

1. **Preference reduction objection**: `d-value` is just strong preference.
2. **Salience reduction objection**: `d-value` is just attention or arousal.
3. **Utility objection**: rational choice theory already captures this.
4. **Moralism objection**: high `d-value` smuggles in moral goodness.
5. **Subjectivism objection**: concern is private and unmeasurable.
6. **Circularity objection**: something matters because it matters.
7. **Overbreadth objection**: every important psychological variable becomes d-value.
8. **Operationalization objection**: no objective scale can measure it.

Required replies:

```text
separate construct from proxy;
state failure conditions;
use multi-proxy convergence;
retain moral legitimacy guardrail;
allow narrowing if baselines explain the data.
```

## 11. Abstract variants

### 11.1 More philosophical

This paper develops `d-value` as an ontology of concern: a way of describing why some contents, relations, losses, and possibilities matter beyond their salience, reward, or utility. In Selective Reality Theory, `d-value` marks concern-weighted non-substitutability and future-selectability impact. It helps explain why certain events become existentially anchored and why some values reorganize agency without being reducible to preference or moral goodness.

### 11.2 More cognitive-scientific

`d-value` is proposed as a construct for distinguishing existential stake from salience, reward, preference, and arousal. The paper argues that high-`d-value` contents should predict memory persistence, action reorganization, and identity impact beyond standard priority and value measures. It outlines proxy measures and failure conditions for testing this hypothesis.

### 11.3 More provocative

Not everything that captures attention matters, and not everything that matters captures attention. This paper introduces `d-value` as the missing distinction: the existential weight of what changes what a subject can become, preserve, lose, or refuse to exchange.
