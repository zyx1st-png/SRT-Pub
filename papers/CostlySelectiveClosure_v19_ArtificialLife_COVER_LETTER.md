# Cover Letter — Artificial Life

**Manuscript:** *Who Bears Failure? Consequence Scope and Terminality in Survival-Coupled Artificial Agents*

**Article type:** Article

Dear Editors,

I am submitting the manuscript **“Who Bears Failure? Consequence Scope and Terminality in Survival-Coupled Artificial Agents”** for consideration as an Article in *Artificial Life*.

The manuscript fits the journal’s scope because it uses a synthetic multi-agent system to investigate an organizational question directly relevant to artificial life: when failure occurs in an artificial agent, which unit loses future action, interaction, or return-bearing opportunity, and how does that architecture affect learned social organization? The paper is not presented as a reinforcement-learning performance study. Reinforcement learning is used as a controlled constructive testbed for comparing failure and recovery architectures that are often described more loosely as mortality, precariousness, vulnerability, or maintenance cost.

The article reports a sequential E1–E4 evidence programme. Experiment 1, which was not preregistered, found a large terminal-versus-restore cooperation difference. Rather than treating that result as confirmation of a preferred theory, two later separately repository-preregistered experiments tested broader interpretations and did not support them: persistent non-terminal metabolic impairment did not reproduce the terminal effect, and individual-scoped recovery latency produced an association opposite the preregistered positive prediction. A subsequent mechanism audit motivated Experiment 4, which was separately preregistered before implementation and confirmatory execution. Holding the non-terminal recovery mechanism fixed, E4 compared individual with shared failure-triggered action-opportunity loss. Shared scope produced a statistically clear but modest positive policy effect (mean shared-minus-individual ≈ +0.030, 95% CI ≈ [+0.012,+0.054]); the preregistered +0.10 strong-effect threshold failed.

The resulting claim is deliberately bounded. The manuscript does **not** claim that shared fate generally produces cooperation, that terminality has been fully explained, that the software agents are literally alive, or that the originating Costly Selective Closure framework has been validated. Prior work already shows that shared/team consequences and interdependent survival can organize cooperation, including Tampuu et al. (2017) and Scott and Pitt (2023). The contribution here is the controlled decomposition of one initially strong terminality effect through two preserved unfavorable preregistered generalization tests and a later preregistered consequence-scope discriminator. The evidence supports consequence scope as a real but limited variable while leaving additional terminality-specific causal structure unresolved.

This combination of constructive modeling, explicit organizational-level distinctions, preregistered discrimination tests, and evidence-led theory revision is intended to speak to *Artificial Life* readers interested in synthetic agency, precariousness, individuality, collective organization, and the methodological interpretation of failure and recovery in artificial systems.

The current manuscript is approximately **7,716 words**, with six keywords, and is substantially unique and not under review elsewhere. Reviewer-facing supplementary materials preserve the fixed result records, preregistration provenance, invariant/adjudication records, figure-generation materials, and code/repository provenance needed to inspect the E1–E4 sequence.
