# Cover Letter — Artificial Life

Dear Editors of *Artificial Life*,

Please consider the manuscript **“Who Bears Failure? Consequence Scope and Terminality in Survival-Coupled Artificial Agents”** for publication as an Article in *Artificial Life*.

The manuscript fits the journal’s scope by using a deliberately minimal artificial-life system to separate several failure architectures that are often grouped together under labels such as mortality, precariousness, vulnerability, or recovery cost. Rather than proposing another general definition of life, the paper asks a narrower comparative question: when failure occurs, which organizational units actually lose future action, interaction, or return opportunities, and does that structural difference alter learning?

The empirical programme contains one discovery experiment followed by three prospectively constrained discrimination tests. Experiment 1 found a large terminal-versus-restore cooperation separation in a two-agent survival-coupled REINFORCE environment, with zero-penalty, lives-budget, payoff-sweep, and frozen-policy common-state checks. Two later repository-preregistered generalizations did not support the original simple intuition: persistent non-terminal metabolic impairment did not produce the predicted cooperation gradient, and increasing individual recovery latency produced an ordinal trend in the opposite direction. These unfavorable results are retained as central evidence rather than treated as failed pilots.

Experiment 4 then preregistered a narrower mechanism discriminator. Holding one non-terminal recovery architecture fixed, it compared individual versus shared failure-triggered action-opportunity loss. On a common 2,000-state frozen-policy endpoint, shared scope increased learned mutual-cooperation propensity by approximately +0.030 on average across latencies (95% CI [0.012, 0.054]; paired sign-flip p = 0.00015), with 29 of 30 latency-collapsed seed contrasts positive. The preregistered +0.10 strong-effect threshold was not reached. The manuscript therefore treats consequence scope as empirically relevant but modest, and explicitly leaves the larger Experiment 1 terminality phenomenon mechanistically unresolved.

I believe the contribution is well suited to *Artificial Life* because the paper’s main result is not the generic observation that shared consequences can support cooperation. That idea already has clear neighbors. The contribution is the controlled E1–E4 decomposition: a strong initial phenomenon, two preregistered failed generalizations, and a later preregistered scope discriminator that progressively separates internal impairment, individual action-opportunity loss, shared recovery consequence, and terminal continuation structure. The resulting consequence descriptor is offered as a methodological discipline for comparing artificial systems, not as a validated ontology or a claim that the present agents are alive.

The manuscript is accompanied by a reviewer evidence/reproduction package containing the relevant code paths, locked preregistrations, committed result records, figure-generation assets, and the preserved first Experiment 4 confirmatory artifact. The review package is prepared as a static upload rather than a permission-gated repository link so that reviewer access cannot reveal reviewer identity to the author.
