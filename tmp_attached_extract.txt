Selection Cost as a Fisher Information Metric:
A Riemannian Geometry of Embodied Updating
Yuxin Zhang
Independent Researcher
Correspondence: zyx1st@gmail.com
Abstract
We formulate embodied model updating as motion on a statistical manifold and define an
operational selection cost (Ψf). Under standard invariance constraints—reparameterization in-
variance and monotonicity under coarse-graining by sufficient statistics—the only monotone
Riemannian metric (up to scale) is the Fisher–Rao information metric; we therefore identify
Ψf ≡ g. This yields a unified geometry for natural-gradient selection dynamics, minimum-
friction (thermodynamic-length) paths, and instability indicators based on curvature and spec-
tral conditioning. A sectional-curvature focusing bound motivates an operational hypothesis:
abrupt reconfiguration episodes in noisy nonconvex learning align with spikes in computable
Fisher-spectrum proxies. Under explicit assumptions about discrimination resolution and irre-
versibility, we derive a scaling lower boundE(d) ≳ d log d linking effective sensitivity dimen-
sionality to energetic budgets (Landauer scale). Two reproducible illustrations—online mixture
adaptation and a moderate-size neural-network regime shift—show that Fisher-spectrum prox-
ies can produce substantially higher standardized shift signals than raw NLL and can support
simple change-point baselines (z-score/CUSUM).
Keywords: Fisher information metric; information geometry; natural gradient; thermodynamic
length; curvature; conjugate points; learning dynamics; embodied inference; irreversibility; Lan-
dauer bound
1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation: Embodied Updating Has a Cost
Finite observers cannot update beliefs, models, or action policies “for free.” Any nontrivial adapta-
tion must be realized through physical processes that are resource-limited and time-asymmetric. We
call the irreducible cost of embodied updatingontological friction: the minimal “commitment cost”
associated with making and maintaining discriminations in a world that does not provide unlimited
reversible computation.
Our goal is to formalize this cost in a way that is (i) operational (estimable from data), (ii) geo-
metrically structured (to admit invariants, optimal paths, and stability criteria), and (iii) bridging
(connecting learning/inference dynamics to thermodynamic/irreversibility constraints).
1.2 Embodied anchoring: from distinguishability to physical cost
A potential weakness of purely statistical readings is the jump from “difficulty of discrimination”
to “cost of existence.” Here we make that bridge explicit. A parameter updateθ → θ + dθ changes
the predictive distribution, and maintaining reliable discrimination under finite resources typically

requires physical work: sampling, memory refresh, actuation, and error-correction against environ-
mental and internal noise. In near-equilibrium control and in information-thermodynamic settings,
the Fisher–Rao metric governs a canonical notion of length and, under suitable assumptions, bounds
excess dissipation along finite-time protocols. This motivates interpretingΨf ≡ g as anoperational
friction/commitment metric: it quantifies the resistance encountered when an embodied system
moves in model space while preserving distinguishability.
To avoid overextending the interpretation, it is useful to separate “inference geometry” from
“embodied anchoring.” We introduce an (operational) embodiment coupling parameterκbody ∈
[0, 1] capturing the degree to which the system’s internal updates are constrained by, and coupled
back to, an external sensorimotor loop. When κbody ≈ 0 (weak coupling; effectively open-loop
simulation), Fisher geometry may still describe statistical sensitivity, but it need not track an
existentially relevant maintenance cost. When κbody > 0, updates that preserve discriminability
must be physically stabilized against entropic pressure, making the cost interpretation substantive.
1.3 Why Information Geometry?
Information geometry equips statistical models with a canonical Riemannian structure: the Fisher–
Rao metric, uniquely invariant (up to scale) under sufficient statistics and reparameterizations.
This geometry has a long history in statistical inference, machine learning, and nonequilibrium
thermodynamics (thermodynamic length and optimal protocols).
Our central move is to interpret this metric not only as “curvature of likelihood,” but as an
operational proxy for embodied selection cost: the resistance a finite system experiences when it
attempts to move in model space while maintaining discriminability and control.
1.4 Relation to Existing Frameworks
This work aligns with (i) natural-gradient learning (Amari), (ii) thermodynamic metrics and op-
timal paths (thermodynamic length; dissipative protocols), and (iii) curvature- or spectrum-based
indicators in complex systems. We emphasize that much of the mathematical structure we use is
established; the present paper’s novelty lies in (a) the interpretive identificationΨf ≡ g, (b) the re-
sulting operational instability hypotheses, and (c) an explicit irreversibility/energy scaling proposal
tied to effective sensitivity dimensionality.

2.1.1 Why Fisher? Operational invariances and Čencov’s uniqueness
A common reviewer question is why the Fisher–Rao metric is the right operational choice forΨf,
ratherthanoneofmanyquadraticformsonparameterspace. Themotivationusedhereisconstraint-
driven:
• Reparameterization invariance:the cost of an infinitesimal update should not depend on
the coordinate chart forθ.
• Coarse-graining / sufficient statistics invariance:if an observer replaces raw data by a
sufficient statistic (or applies a stochastic map that preserves all information relevant toθ),
the intrinsic update-cost geometry should not becomemore discriminating.
• Locality: for small updates, the cost should admit a second-order expansion induced by a
smooth divergence between nearby distributions.
Related divergence-geometry constructions andα-geometries provide complementary perspectives
on how local quadratic forms arise from statistical divergences (e.g., Burbea & Rao [23]; Amari [22]).
Under these conditions, information geometry provides a uniqueness result: Čencov’s theorem
states that the only Riemannian metric on the statistical simplex that is monotone under stochastic
maps is the Fisher information metric, up to an overall constant factor. This supports treating
Ψf ≡ g as an operational identification anchored in invariance and data-processing constraints,
rather than an arbitrary axiom.

3.2 Degenerate-metric limitation (conditional)
Theorem 4 (T-IG-4; conditional limitation). If, for a system’s online-adaptive degrees of
freedom, the effectiveΨf collapses to near rank-zero (or near-zero spectrum under operational esti-
mation), then within this framework the system admits no nontrivial friction geometry supporting
sustained embodied selection in those degrees of freedom. Consequently, any reconfiguration criteria
based on Fisher-geometry proxies become inapplicable or predict low values under perturbational
complexity measures adapted to the substrate. This is a conditional limitation statement about the
representational scope of this criterion, not a metaphysical exclusion across all possible substrates.

4 Discussion
4.1 Scope of the identification Ψf ≡ g
A recurring epistemic concern is whetherΨf ≡ g is an axiom, an empirical hypothesis, or a unique-
ness claim. Section 2.1.1 makes the intended status explicit via invariance and data-processing con-
straints (Čencov uniqueness). In this paper we treat it as anoperational identificationmotivated by
(i) the canonical role of Fisher–Rao geometry in statistical distinguishability and (ii) its appearance
as a dissipation/length metric in finite-time control and thermodynamic-length formalisms. The
identification should not be read as a claim thatall Fisher information is existentially relevant;
rather, the cost interpretation is substantive when updates areembodiedly anchored(κbody > 0)
and must be physically stabilized against noise.
A useful organizing picture is astabilization degreeκ ∈ [0, 1] describing how strongly a system’s
internal selection operator stabilizes a manifest model state against latent variability. Near critical
stabilization regimes (intermediate κ), Fisher geometry is expected to be most informative about
selection cost because small parameter moves can induce large changes in discriminability and
control. Far from such regimes, the effective metric may either collapse on the online-adaptive
subspace (consistent with the degenerate-metric limitation in Theorem 4) or become approximately

Operationalizing κbody. In the present numerical experiments, the learner is fully coupled to

errors incur irreversible physiological costs (metabolic or structural), and (iii) cross-modal constraint
strength between interoceptive and exteroceptive channels. Clarifying an operational measurement
of κbody is an important target for empirical follow-up.
4.2 Curvature proxies, noise, and “insight”
The focusing bound provides a sufficient geometric condition for loss of local optimality. In noisy
nonconvex systems this is predicted to elevate basin-switch probabilities. Because sectional curva-
ture is rarely estimable in high dimensions, our hypotheses are formulated in terms of measurable
proxies: Fisher-spectrum reorganization, conditioning spikes, and (in dual-stream settings) cross-
Fisher restructuring.
4.3 Scalability and computational trade-offs
A practical concern is whether Fisher-spectrum proxies remain useful in higher-dimensional param-
eter spaces (e.g., CNNs on CIFAR-scale tasks), and what their computational footprint is. Naïvely,
forming an empirical Fisher requires storing per-sample gradients inRP and has time complexity
O(BP ) per probe step for batch sizeB and parameter countP. Our experiments exploit the Gram
trick: the nonzero eigenvalues of(1/B)G⊤G equal those of S = (1 /B)GG⊤ ∈ RB×B, so leading
spectral quantities can be extracted without explicitly materializing aP × P matrix. Nevertheless,
computing G still scales with backprop cost and withP.
For larger models, several approximations are standard: (i)structured curvature(e.g., K-FAC
and related Kronecker/ block-diagonal approximations) reduces both storage and inversion cost
while retaining meaningful spectral information [13]; (ii) stochastic trace/eigenvalue estimators
(Hutchinson trace; stochastic Lanczos) estimatetr(Ψf) or leading eigenvalues using Jacobian–vector
products; (iii) layerwise or blockwise proxiestrack spectra within selected modules; and (iv)sub-
sampled probestrade variance for speed.
Empirical studies suggest that Fisher spectra in deep networks are highly anisotropic, with many
