# Selective Reality Theory as a Candidate Translation Framework for Consciousness Science

## Abstract

Consciousness science continues to generate influential theories without approaching stable theoretical convergence. Using Robert Lawrence Kuhn's recent landscape of consciousness as a starting point, this paper argues that at least part of this proliferation reflects a structural problem: major theories often target different explanatory dimensions of consciousness rather than offering directly commensurable accounts of a single level of description. I propose that Selective Reality Theory (SRT) is most useful when read not as a further first-order theory of consciousness, but as a candidate translation framework for relating these dimensions within a shared comparative vocabulary. On this interpretation, SRT distinguishes latent conditions, manifest experiential organization, and convergent public or normative structure, and uses this triadic contrast to reinterpret recurrent tensions among IIT, global workspace theory, predictive processing/free energy approaches, panpsychism, idealism, and challenge theories. I then introduce the notion of existential stake (`d`) as a provisional construct intended to capture the depth and scope of concern-sensitive selection, and sketch several empirical hypotheses---including cross-theoretical predictions unique to the translation framework---that could help test whether it adds explanatory value beyond existing structural measures. I conclude by identifying the framework's current limits, its relation to the stronger formulation developed elsewhere, and the conditions under which it should be revised or rejected.

**Keywords:** consciousness, theory comparison, metatheory, Selective Reality Theory, Integrated Information Theory, global workspace theory, predictive processing, explanatory pluralism

## 1 Introduction: The Problem of Theoretical Proliferation

Consciousness science is now rich in theories but poor in convergence. This is usually treated as a sign either of premature theory formation or of deep unresolved disagreement about the nature of consciousness itself. In this paper I explore a third possibility: that at least part of the field's persistent fragmentation reflects a mismatch between explanatory targets. Some theories primarily describe access and reportability, some structural integration, some adaptive regulation, and some the metaphysical status of experience. If so, non-convergence may not simply indicate that most theories are false, but that they are often speaking past one another. Using Robert Lawrence Kuhn's recent taxonomy as a starting point, I argue that Selective Reality Theory (SRT) is most productively read as a candidate translation framework for comparing these explanatory targets, rather than as a further first-order theory competing to replace them (R. L. Kuhn, 2024; Seth and Bayne, 2022).

Kuhn's survey is especially useful because it makes visible the scale of the problem. Contemporary consciousness studies no longer presents a small number of rival grand theories, but a dense landscape of partially overlapping proposals distributed across physicalist, informational, computational, phenomenological, idealist, panpsychist, dualist, and challenge-oriented traditions (R. L. Kuhn, 2024). This plurality has often been narrated as an embarrassment for the field. Yet it may also reveal something important: theories of consciousness are frequently strongest when they are local. They perform well when answering a particular kind of question, under a particular ontology, with a particular class of evidence, but become unstable when extended beyond that domain.

Recent debate supports this diagnosis. IIT and global workspace theory remain among the best-developed frameworks in the field, yet the adversarial comparison literature has not simply produced a winner. Instead, it has repeatedly suggested that theories succeed on partly different dimensions, at different temporal and functional scales, and under different assumptions about what a successful theory ought to explain (Tononi et al., 2016; Dehaene et al., 2017; Melloni et al., 2023). Something similar holds for predictive processing and free-energy approaches, which provide unusually powerful accounts of adaptive regulation while leaving open the relation between regulation, access, and phenomenality (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013; Limanowski and Friston, 2018).

The same pattern extends beyond neuroscience-centered theories. Panpsychism, idealism, dualism, and challenge theories do not merely compete over the same causal model. They intervene at the level of metaphysical priority, explanatory adequacy, or the limits of cognition itself (Chalmers, 1995; McGinn, 1989; Strawson, 2006; Goff, 2019; Kastrup, 2019). A field containing disagreements of all these kinds should not be expected to converge through one more local experimental result alone. It needs ways of distinguishing disagreements about evidence from disagreements about scope, and disagreements about scope from disagreements about ontology.

Recent debate over whether some prominent theories count as scientific, pseudoscientific, or insufficiently testable pushes the point even further. The field is now arguing not only about which theory is best supported, but also about what standards of appraisal should govern theory choice in the first place (Negro, 2024; Gomez-Marin and Seth, 2025). That is precisely the kind of pressure under which translation-oriented and comparison-oriented work becomes useful.

The claim of this paper is therefore modest but consequential. I do not argue that SRT settles the problem of consciousness, provides a new physical foundation, or replaces existing theories. I argue instead that SRT may be useful as a comparative vocabulary for relating why major theories succeed where they do, why they fail to converge globally, and where new empirical discriminations might be found. On this reading, SRT functions as a translation framework: its value depends not on whether it terminates the debate, but on whether it clarifies the structure of disagreement and helps generate more discriminating research questions.

The paper proceeds in six steps. First, I argue that much apparent rivalry in consciousness science reflects mismatched explanatory burdens rather than simple contradiction. Second, I reformulate the core SRT distinctions in a deliberately weaker and more comparative register. Third, I introduce existential stake (`d`) as a provisional construct intended to capture concern-sensitive organization that may be under-described by existing structural frameworks. Fourth, I use this vocabulary to reread several major theory families, showing concretely what the translation reveals and where it encounters limits. Fifth, I outline empirical consequences, including cross-theoretical predictions unique to the translation framework. Finally, I identify conditions of failure and clarify the relation between this weaker reading and the stronger formulation developed elsewhere.

## 2 Why Local Success Does Not Yield Global Convergence

The persistence of theory proliferation suggests that current disagreements are not merely empirical. Integrated Information Theory, global workspace theory, predictive processing and the free energy principle, higher-order theories, and various non-physicalist approaches do not simply offer different answers to the same narrowly specified question. They often operate at different explanatory levels. IIT is strongest when describing structural irreducibility. Global workspace theory is strongest when describing broadcast, access, and report. Predictive processing and the free energy principle are strongest when describing adaptive regulation and model-based action. Panpsychist, idealist, and dualist positions intervene at the level of ontological priority. Challenge theories such as mysterianism diagnose the limits of explanation itself.

### 2.1 Explanatory Burden Mismatch

One reason consciousness theories fail to converge is that they are often asked to carry more burden than they were built to bear. A framework may be developed to explain one cluster of findings and then be criticized for not solving an adjacent problem that lies outside its natural scope. Global workspace theory, for example, has always been especially strong on access, report, and large-scale broadcasting dynamics (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 2017). Its critics are often right that this does not by itself explain why such broadcasting should be accompanied by experience. But that criticism does not show that the theory fails on its own home terrain; it shows that access and phenomenality cannot be assumed to be identical explanatory tasks.

IIT illustrates the complementary problem. It offers one of the most ambitious attempts to characterize the structural conditions under which a system is more or less unified from its own intrinsic perspective (Tononi et al., 2016; Albantakis et al., 2023). Yet even sympathetic readers worry that the theory's formal richness outruns its empirical tractability, and that its implications may be too liberal when detached from embodiment, function, and context. Again, this does not show that IIT has no target. It shows that a theory optimized for irreducibility and intrinsic structure may not automatically resolve questions about report, adaptive significance, or phenomenological differentiation in the same way.

The ongoing IIT-GWT dispute makes the point especially clearly. In polemical form, the dispute is often framed as a direct fight over the neural basis of consciousness. In practice, however, each theory prioritizes a different explanatory axis. IIT foregrounds integrated causal structure. GWT foregrounds ignition, global availability, and flexible access. When experimental adversarial work yields mixed rather than decisive results, one possible lesson is simply that one theory is wrong. Another is that the experiment was asking two frameworks with partly different targets to compete as if they were addressing exactly the same explanatory burden (Melloni et al., 2023). The recent large-scale COGITATE report strengthens this point: its results challenged important commitments of both IIT and GNWT, while showing that the path forward may require more systematic theory testing and better tools for comparing predictions across frameworks (Cogitate Consortium et al., 2025).

### 2.2 Incommensurability Is Real, but Not Total

There is, however, a danger in pushing this diagnosis too far. If every theory is said to target something different, then real disagreement disappears and theory choice becomes impossible. That conclusion would be too easy. Some disagreements are genuine and substantive. Physicalism and idealism are not simply different reporting languages. Panpsychism and illusionism do not merely describe adjacent aspects of the same mechanism. A translation framework therefore has to do two things at once: it must acknowledge partial incommensurability without turning all contradiction into complementarity.

This is where the more modest use of SRT begins. It does not ask us to treat all theories as equivalent. It asks whether a small comparative vocabulary can help distinguish at least three recurring moments in consciousness theories: what a theory treats as the background conditions of possible experience, what it treats as the organization of a presently manifest conscious state, and what it treats as the stable structures by which such states become reproducible, shareable, or publicly trackable. The utility of SRT depends on whether these distinctions clarify debate without erasing it.

### 2.3 Why a Translation Framework Is Worth Trying

Once these differences are seen clearly, the failure of convergence becomes less mysterious. A theory that successfully characterizes the functional organization associated with access need not settle the metaphysical status of subjectivity. A theory that offers a mathematically elegant account of integration need not explain why some integrated systems are conscious while others are not. A theory that explains adaptive self-maintenance need not thereby explain phenomenal character. In this sense, many disputes in consciousness science are not simple winner-take-all contests; they are disagreements between frameworks optimized for different explanatory burdens.

This diagnosis motivates the search for a translation framework. Such a framework should not collapse distinctions too quickly, and it should not pretend that all theories are saying the same thing. Its task is more modest: to show where apparently rival theories may be locally compatible, where their explanatory targets differ, and where genuine contradictions remain.

This is not an idiosyncratic demand generated only by SRT. Recent proposals in consciousness science have already moved toward more explicit comparison frameworks, including Lakatosian models of theory appraisal and attempts to formalize how central particular measures are to different theories (Negro, 2024; Chis-Ciure et al., 2024). The present paper belongs to that family of efforts, though it proceeds through a more qualitative comparative vocabulary. Its specific contribution, relative to those proposals, will be clarified after the framework is introduced.

## 3 SRT as a Candidate Translation Framework

Read as a first-order metaphysics, SRT appears overly ambitious. Read as a translation framework, its central move is simpler. It distinguishes among three recurrent moments in consciousness talk: conditions that make a reality slice available, the organization of a presently manifest experiential or operational state, and the stabilization of that state into shared patterns, norms, or public structure. In the original SRT vocabulary these are rendered as `L0`, `L1`, and `L2`. For present purposes, however, their importance lies less in their metaphysical finality than in the comparative work they allow us to do.

### 3.1 The Triadic Distinction in a Weaker Register

`L0` can be treated as a label for latent possibility space: whatever background of unrealized or underdetermined structure a theory assumes when it explains why one experiential organization emerges rather than another. `L1` names the currently manifest organization that a theory takes to be directly relevant to conscious experience, access, or behavior. `L2` marks stabilized convergence: the structures, models, or shared constraints through which patterns become reproducible, publicly tractable, or normatively organized. This triadic distinction is useful because major theories of consciousness often privilege one of these moments while downplaying the others.

The important thing here is not the labels themselves. The point is to ask three comparative questions of any theory. First, what does the theory presuppose as the space of possible organization from which conscious states are drawn or selected? Second, what does it treat as the organization of a presently conscious state? Third, what stabilizes that organization across time, contexts, or observers? Many theories answer only one or two of these questions explicitly. That is not necessarily a defect. But it helps explain why they can appear more or less complete depending on what kind of explanatory demand is being made of them.

There is a useful empirical parallel here. Pérez et al. (2024) argue that neuronal markers of consciousness can be organized along partially distinct state and content dimensions, rather than treated as a single undifferentiated class of markers. Their proposal is not the same as SRT, but it illustrates the broader value of explicit conceptual dissociation. A comparative framework earns credibility when its distinctions resonate with real explanatory and measurement differences already visible in the literature.

### 3.2 How Translation Is Supposed to Work

The point of the triadic vocabulary is not to show that reality literally consists of three ontological layers. The point is to supply a compact comparative grammar. Once expressed this way, some longstanding disputes become easier to diagnose. Theories centered on neural mechanisms often privilege manifest implementation. Structural theories tend to privilege formal organization. Predictive and free-energy approaches emphasize adaptive updating and stability conditions. Panpsychist and idealist views often intervene on what should count as ontologically primary. SRT is useful insofar as it makes these differences easier to compare without prematurely collapsing them.

More concretely, translation here means three things. It means locating a theory's strongest explanatory target, making explicit what the theory leaves in the background, and clarifying whether its central concepts are being used mechanistically, structurally, phenomenologically, or metaphysically. This matters because consciousness debates are unusually vulnerable to cross-level equivocation. A term such as "integration," "access," "self-model," or "appearance" can shift its role within an argument without the shift being clearly marked.

Under this weaker reading, SRT is not an operating system in the strong sense of a master framework into which all theories must be compiled. It is closer to a translation manual that asks what kind of work each theory is doing, which distinctions it sharpens, and which distinctions it blurs. Its success therefore depends on interpretive discipline rather than totalizing reach.

### 3.3 What This Framework Adds Beyond Existing Comparison Tools

It is fair to ask how this triadic vocabulary improves on existing comparison efforts. Seth and Bayne (2022) distinguish theories along dimensions such as substrate, mechanism, and relation to the physical; Chis-Ciure et al. (2024) formalize how central particular measures are to particular theories. Both approaches are valuable. The present framework differs from them in one specific way: it does not compare theories primarily by asking what each says about consciousness, but by asking what *moment of a generative process* each theory primarily describes. This shift matters because it reframes apparent contradictions as potentially complementary descriptions of different stages.

Consider the IIT-GWT dispute. Compared along the usual dimensions---neural substrate, mechanism, scope---IIT and GWT appear to make rival claims about where consciousness "lives" in the brain. Compared within the triadic vocabulary, they appear as theories optimized for different moments: IIT for the structural capacity of the manifest organization, GWT for the transition event by which latent processing becomes manifest. This is not a resolution. But it generates a specific empirical question that neither theory alone would formulate: Can structural capacity and transition dynamics be experimentally dissociated? If so, it would be a genuinely new finding emerging from the comparative vocabulary rather than from either theory in isolation (see Section 6.2 for development of this point).

More generally, the triadic grammar generates a diagnostic checklist for any theory of consciousness: What does it say about latent conditions? About manifest organization? About stabilization? Where a theory is silent on one of these three, the silence is itself informative---it identifies the point at which the theory's explanatory power gives way to assumption.

### 3.4 Why This Is Not Yet a Replacement Theory

This weaker reading also imposes clear constraints. SRT should not claim that because it can redescribe another theory, it has therefore explained that theory's evidence better than the original authors. Nor should it claim that a triadic restatement automatically resolves disagreements about measurement, neural implementation, or metaphysical truth. A translation framework is useful only if it leaves room for the continued autonomy of the translated theories.

That is why the current paper intentionally avoids the strongest original SRT claims about universal ontology, completed formal proof, or final consciousness conditions. Those stronger claims may be defensible elsewhere, but they are not required for the present task. The task here is comparative clarification. If the framework cannot justify its place at that lower and more vulnerable level, there is little reason to demand acceptance at a higher one.

## 4 Existential Stake as a Provisional Construct

The most distinctive part of the SRT proposal is the notion of existential stake, or `d`. In the stronger version of the theory, `d` is treated as a substrate-independent measure with broad formal significance (Zhang, 2026). For the present paper, that stronger claim is bracketed. Here `d` should be read more cautiously as a provisional construct intended to capture something that many major theories treat only indirectly: the depth and scope of concern-sensitive selection.

### 4.1 Why Introduce `d` at All?

The intuition behind `d` is straightforward. A system may display rich structural integration, complex information processing, or sophisticated behavioral organization, yet still differ dramatically from another system in how much of the world genuinely matters to its continued organization or self-maintenance. This difference may be relevant to consciousness debates because some structural measures appear insufficient to distinguish between a merely integrated process and a process for which ongoing organization is existentially consequential. The `d` construct is an attempt to mark that gap.

The motivation for introducing `d` is not that existing theories are empty. On the contrary, many of them already contain partial analogues. In enactive and embodied approaches, concern is bound up with sense-making and the organism's coupling to its world (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). In predictive approaches, the organism's continued existence and model-guided self-maintenance are central. In everyday phenomenology, some situations matter to the subject in a way that is not captured by complexity alone---Nagel's question of "what it is like" already gestures toward this (Nagel, 1974). The proposal behind `d` is that these concern-sensitive dimensions may deserve a more explicit place in theory comparison.

Two clarifications matter. First, `d` is not identical to intelligence. A system may solve difficult tasks while remaining narrow in what genuinely matters to its continued organization. Second, `d` is not simply equivalent to biological life. Biology may often be a powerful carrier of stake-sensitive organization, but the point of the construct is comparative, not taxonomic.

### 4.2 What `d` Is Meant to Track and How It Differs from Nearby Constructs

In the weaker version adopted here, `d` refers to the depth and breadth of stake-sensitive organization. Depth concerns how strongly a system's ongoing organization depends on particular outcomes, relations, or losses. Breadth concerns how many dimensions, timescales, or others are included within that field of concern. A system with narrow and shallow stake may still be highly organized. A system with broader and deeper stake may display forms of persistence, flexibility, or vulnerability that are difficult to read off from structural measures alone.

The most obvious neighboring construct is utility. Why not simply say that `d` is the number of things a system values? The answer is that utility, at least in its formal use, is too permissive. A utility function can be assigned to almost any optimizing description, including systems for which nothing is at issue from the system's own continued organization. `d` is intended to be narrower. It points not to abstract preference ordering alone, but to concern whose breakdown alters the system's own mode of organization, persistence, or self-maintenance.

A subtler neighboring construct is the notion of "preferred priors" within free-energy frameworks. One might argue that what `d` calls existential stake is simply a set of strong prior expectations that the system is built to maintain. This reduction deserves serious consideration. It would fail, however, if the following distinction can be sustained: preferred priors specify *what* a system is set to expect, while `d` indexes *how much of the world* constitutes genuine stakes for the system's continued coherent organization. Two systems might share identical precision parameters and expected free energy while differing in how broadly and deeply their ongoing organization is coupled to external states---one responding only to local metabolic perturbation, the other maintaining coherence across temporal, social, and self-other dimensions. If this distinction is not empirically real, `d` is reducible to precision. If it is real, `d` marks something that free-energy frameworks describe only indirectly.

Nor should `d` be treated as a replacement for structural complexity. The point of the construct is orthogonality, not supremacy. A system could in principle score high on some complexity measure yet remain thin in stake-sensitive organization. Another system might be relatively simpler but exhibit stronger persistence of concern under loss, disruption, or delayed reward. If such dissociations cannot be found, then `d` is either redundant or poorly specified. The construct is worth keeping only if the dissociation is real.

### 4.3 A Minimal Operational Stance

At this stage the paper does not need a maximal formal proof of `d`. It needs a disciplined proposal. The central question is whether a stake-sensitive construct adds explanatory value beyond existing structural proxies. If it does, `d` merits a place in the comparative vocabulary. If it does not, the construct should be revised or abandoned. The right standard here is not metaphysical elegance but empirical and conceptual payoff.

For that reason, I suggest a minimal operational stance rather than a definitive metric. A provisional proxy for existential stake might combine four components summarized in Table 1. None of these measures is sufficient on its own, and each is theory-laden. But together they sketch the sort of empirical profile that would make `d` more than a metaphor.

| Proxy dimension | Guiding question | Example operationalization | Established paradigm |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Temporal horizon | How far ahead does the system continue to organize around possible outcomes? | Delay discounting slope; planning depth | Intertemporal choice tasks (Green and Myerson, 2004) |
| Breadth of concern | How wide is the field of states, others, or relations treated as non-trivial? | Self-other inclusion tasks; social concern span | Inclusion of Other in Self scale (Aron et al., 1992); social discounting |
| Persistence under reward loss | Does organized behavior survive short-term withdrawal of explicit reinforcement? | Goal persistence after reward removal | Extinction paradigms; intrinsic motivation tasks (Deci and Ryan, 1985) |
| Cost sensitivity across self/other tradeoffs | How does organization change when preserving other-relevant states becomes personally costly? | Costly helping or costly coordination paradigms | Dictator game variants; costly punishment (Fehr and Gächter, 2002) |

*Table 1. Minimal proxy dimensions for a provisional measure of existential stake, with established paradigm references.*

The empirical strategy should be incremental. First, establish whether the stake-sensitive variables correlate strongly enough with one another to form a meaningful construct at all. Second, test whether that construct is separable from existing complexity measures. Third, evaluate whether the combined model improves prediction across cases. At each stage, failure should count as real failure, not as evidence that the construct has become deeper and more elusive.

## 5 Translating Major Theory Families

On this narrower reading, SRT can be used to reinterpret several major theory families without claiming to subsume them. The purpose of the present section is not merely to assign labels but to show concretely what the triadic vocabulary reveals about each theory's scope, what it identifies as structurally under-described, and where it encounters genuine limits.

### 5.1 IIT and Structural Theories

Within the present framework, IIT is read as a theory that privileges one central feature of manifest organization: intrinsic causal or informational integration (Tononi et al., 2016; Albantakis et al., 2023). In triadic terms, IIT is strongest when describing a structural aspect of the currently manifest state---namely how unified or irreducible a present organization is from its own internal perspective. This reading preserves IIT's explanatory strength without demanding that the theory also solve every problem of access, embodiment, and adaptive significance.

This translation also clarifies why critics so often press IIT on liberal attribution. IIT's five postulates (intrinsicality, information, integration, exclusion, composition) characterize what makes a physical substrate capable of supporting consciousness from its own intrinsic perspective. In triadic terms, these postulates describe the *structural capacity* of the manifest organization. What they do not directly address is the *selection process* by which one particular manifest state is realized from among the possible states, nor the *stabilization* by which that realization becomes reproducible. In other words, IIT provides a powerful characterization of `L1` architecture while leaving `L0`-to-`L1` transition dynamics and `L2` convergence largely unspecified.

This is not a critique of IIT. It is a diagnosis of where IIT's home terrain ends and adjacent problems begin. The construct `d` enters here not as an anti-IIT weapon, but as a candidate complement: a way of asking whether integrated organization and stake-sensitive organization are empirically dissociable. If they are always perfectly correlated, the distinction adds nothing. If they can be pulled apart---for instance, in dissociative conditions where structural integration is relatively preserved while concern-sensitive organization narrows---then the triadic vocabulary has identified a real joint in nature that IIT's own framework does not carve.

### 5.2 Global Workspace Theory and Access Accounts

Global workspace theory is naturally located elsewhere in the comparative map. Its core strength is not intrinsic structure but the functional profile of global availability, broadcasting, and flexible report (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 2017). In the triadic vocabulary, this makes it especially powerful at describing one specific moment: the *transition* from locally processed signals to globally available manifest states. The "ignition" event---the sudden shift from localized to distributed activity---maps naturally onto a transition from latent processing to manifest organization.

Seen this way, the classical criticism of GWT can be stated more precisely. The problem is not that access is irrelevant to consciousness. The problem is that a theory of access describes the *event* of becoming manifest without fully characterizing either the structural conditions that make that event possible (IIT's home terrain) or the stabilization processes that make it reproducible (a question about convergent structure). A broadcast theory tells us that a state has become globally available. It does not, by itself, explain what makes the difference between a globally broadcast state that is experienced and a globally broadcast state that is merely functionally usable. The translation framework preserves GWT's local success while making the boundary of that success explicit.

This diagnosis also reframes the IIT-GWT rivalry. IIT asks: "What structural properties must a system have for conscious experience to be possible?" GWT asks: "What functional event marks the transition from unconscious processing to conscious access?" These are logically independent questions. A system can possess the structural properties (high integration) without currently undergoing the transition event (deep sleep). Conversely, a system can undergo a transition event (epileptic ignition) without possessing the structural properties (well-integrated causal architecture). The triadic vocabulary does not settle which question is more fundamental. But it makes the independence visible, and that visibility already generates an empirical consequence (see Section 6.2).

### 5.3 Predictive Processing and Free-Energy Approaches

Predictive and free-energy approaches occupy a different but overlapping space in the map. Their natural strength lies in describing how organisms maintain coherence under uncertainty, minimize error, update models, and preserve themselves as adaptive systems (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013). In the triadic vocabulary, they primarily describe the *regulation and updating* of manifest organization under conditions of ongoing environmental coupling---that is, the dynamics by which `L2` parameters are maintained and revised. This gives them unusually strong leverage over questions of action, perception, and self-maintenance.

Here the `d` construct has a particularly interesting relation to the existing literature. The free-energy framework already contains a rich account of how organisms persist---through prediction error minimization, active inference, and expected free energy. But the breadth and depth of what a system takes to be at stake in its ongoing self-maintenance may not be fully captured by precision-weighting and policy selection alone. Consider two systems with identical precision profiles and expected free energy: one is a bacterium maintaining chemotactic gradients, the other is a human deliberating about the welfare of a distant stranger. The formal apparatus of free-energy minimization applies to both. But the *scope* of concern-sensitive coupling---how many dimensions, timescales, and external states are drawn into the system's ongoing organization---differs enormously. The question is whether that difference in scope is merely a quantitative parameter within the existing framework or whether it marks a qualitative distinction that the framework leaves structurally implicit.

One important test case is the "dark room problem"---why organisms do not simply minimize surprise by retreating into sensory deprivation. Standard responses appeal to the organism's expectations about its own continued active engagement with the world. In the triadic vocabulary, this translates as the claim that for systems with broad concern-sensitive coupling, isolation narrows `d` in a way that destabilizes the very organization that needs to be maintained. If `d` is reducible to preferred priors, this adds nothing. If it captures a dimension that preferred priors describe only obliquely, the translation has done real work.

This reading also applies to more recent integrative proposals. Safron's Integrated World Modeling Theory (IWMT) attempts to unify IIT, GWT, and FEP within a single computational architecture based on active inference and world-modeling (Safron, 2020). In triadic terms, IWMT provides a sophisticated account of how manifest organization is stabilized through multi-scale generative models---essentially describing the computational mechanics of `L2` formation. The present framework's relation to IWMT is complementary: IWMT describes how convergent structure is built and maintained; the triadic vocabulary asks what that description leaves out (the `L0`-to-`L1` transition dynamics and the scope of existential coupling that no amount of world-modeling addresses directly).

### 5.4 Higher-Order, Enactive, and Embodied Views

Higher-order theories, enactive approaches, and embodied cognition are especially useful for showing why the map cannot remain purely structural.

Higher-order theories locate the crucial transition not in first-order representation alone but in meta-access or reflexive availability (Rosenthal, 2005). In triadic terms, this amounts to claiming that consciousness requires not just manifest organization (`L1`) but a second-order selection operating *on* that organization---a selection of the selection, so to speak. This is valuable because it identifies a recursive dimension that flat structural theories may miss. But it also introduces the problem of regress: what makes the second-order selection conscious rather than the first? The translation framework does not solve this problem. It clarifies it by asking whether the regress is better described as an infinite tower of meta-representations or as a single selection process whose parameters include its own prior states.

Enactive and embodied approaches insist that cognition and experience cannot be cleanly detached from the organism's practical coupling to the world (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). This insistence maps naturally onto the claim that the manifest organization (`L1`) cannot be characterized independently of the embodied parameters through which the selection operates. In formal SRT vocabulary, this is the Embodiment Necessity axiom: every selection operator possesses finite, materially instantiated parameters. The enactive tradition therefore does not occupy a separate region of the map; it constrains how every region is described. A translation framework that ignored this constraint would fail exactly where enactivism is strongest.

The introduction of existential stake is partly intended to keep this enactive pressure in view. It registers that conscious organization may be inseparable from the organism's field of practical concern, not only from the complexity of its internal state.

### 5.5 Non-Physicalist Theories: Panpsychism, Idealism, Dualism

Panpsychism, idealism, and dualism enter the map at a different level. Their central intervention is not usually to specify a neural mechanism or a broadcast architecture. It is to challenge what should count as ontologically basic, explanatorily sufficient, or metaphysically coherent (Chalmers, 1995; Strawson, 2006; Goff, 2019; Kastrup, 2019). In the triadic vocabulary, these views frequently privilege what lies "beneath" or "behind" manifest organization: the conditions under which experience is even thinkable as something fundamental, distributed, or irreducible to mechanism. This is precisely where translation becomes hardest, and where the framework's limits must be most honestly acknowledged.

**Panpsychism** correctly identifies a continuity in how selection-like processes operate across physical scales. In the language of the present framework, panpsychism's insight is that the latent conditions (`L0`) and minimal selection dynamics are not restricted to biological systems. The triadic vocabulary can accommodate this insight without accepting panpsychism's further claim that all such systems are thereby *conscious*. The distinction between selection activity (which may be ubiquitous) and phenomenal consciousness (which requires additional structural, stake-sensitive, and organizational conditions) provides a way of preserving panpsychism's continuity claim while denying its universality claim. Whether this distinction is ultimately viable depends on whether the additional conditions can be independently motivated---precisely the empirical question raised by the `d` construct. But even as a diagnostic tool, the translation makes explicit *where* panpsychism's reasoning goes from insight to overextension: at the point where "this system exhibits selection-like dynamics" slides into "this system has experience."

**Idealism** makes a different but equally important intervention. It insists that experiential processes are ontologically prior to physical structure---that "to be is to be experienced" rather than the reverse (Kastrup, 2019). In the triadic vocabulary, idealism privileges the selection process itself as primary, treating the manifest domain as more fundamental than the latent conditions from which it is drawn. The present framework can register this priority claim without adjudicating it: if one reads `L0` as parasitic on `L1` rather than the other way around, one gets something close to an idealist interpretation of the triadic structure. What idealism leaves under-described, from the triadic perspective, is the *dynamics* of the selection process---how experience differentiates over time, how it coordinates across observers, and what stabilizes it into shared public structure. Idealism declares the primacy of experience but often struggles to provide a dynamical account of its internal organization.

**Dualism** captures a real structural feature of the triadic map: there is a genuine discontinuity between the latent conditions and the manifest organization. In the triadic vocabulary, the `L0`/`L1` boundary is not an epistemic illusion but a topological feature---manifest states are not simply a subset of latent conditions but are constructed through a selection process that involves thermodynamic cost. Dualism's insight is that this boundary is real. Its error, from the triadic perspective, is to reify a *topological distinction* (a boundary crossed by a dynamical process) into an *ontological dichotomy* (two fundamentally different kinds of substance). The translation framework does not prove dualism wrong. It identifies precisely where dualism's reasoning shifts from structural observation to metaphysical commitment, making the shift easier to evaluate.

It would be a mistake to treat these views as merely confused forms of mechanism talk. But it would also be a mistake to treat their metaphysical force as itself a mechanism. The translation framework helps by making the *kind* of intervention each theory performs more explicit, without pretending to settle whether that intervention is ultimately correct.

### 5.6 Anomalous States and Challenge Theories

**Anomalous and altered states of consciousness**---including meditative absorption, psychedelic ego dissolution, and near-death experiences---provide important test cases because they seem to involve reorganization of the manifest state in ways that challenge normal structural descriptions. In the triadic vocabulary, these states may be understood as conditions in which the relative weighting between external input and internally generated organization shifts dramatically. Deep meditation may involve a narrowing of external input coupling while preserving or even expanding access to latent organizational possibilities. Psychedelic states may involve a rapid destabilization of convergent structure (`L2`), allowing latent conditions to flood manifest organization more directly. This is not a theory of these states; it is a comparative redescription that may help connect phenomenological reports with neuroscientific findings such as those on relaxed beliefs under psychedelics (Carhart-Harris and Friston, 2019).

**Challenge theories** such as mysterianism and illusionism play a distinct and important role. They remind the field that not every gap can be closed by redescribing it. A translation framework that cannot accommodate the possibility of failure quickly becomes self-protective. In the triadic vocabulary, mysterianism can be restated as the claim that our symbolic systems (`L2`) lack the bandwidth to compress the full dimensionality of manifest experience (`L1`). This restatement does not dissolve the challenge. But it transforms it from a claim about absolute unknowability into a claim about compression ratio---which at least in principle admits of degree and improvement. Whether that transformation is genuine progress or merely verbal remains to be seen.

The following table summarizes the translation, not as a final ontological map, but as a comparative tool for locating each theory family's primary explanatory target and the adjacent territory it leaves structurally under-described.

## 6 Empirical Payoff

A metatheoretical paper justifies itself only if it sharpens empirical questions rather than floating above them. The present section distinguishes two kinds of empirical consequence: those that test whether the `d` construct adds value (Section 6.1--6.3), and those that emerge specifically from the cross-theoretical translation (Section 6.4).

### 6.1 Why PCI Is the Right Starting Point

One plausible test domain is the relation between perturbational complexity and conscious state. Existing work on measures such as PCI has provided one of the most important cross-condition tools in consciousness science because it gives a way of tracking complexity while reducing dependence on overt report (Casali et al., 2013; Massimini and Tononi, 2018). A translation-framework reading suggests that this structural dimension may be powerful but incomplete. If so, one would expect some conditions in which structural complexity is relatively preserved while concern-sensitive organization narrows, and other conditions in which concern-sensitive organization recovers on a different time course from structural complexity.

Recent work supports both the promise and the caution required here. Pérez et al. (2024) show that putative neuronal markers can sort differently along state and content dimensions. Xu et al. (2024) report that PCIst tracked responsiveness and prognosis in a randomized controlled rTMS study of minimally conscious patients. Any proposed stake-sensitive construct must therefore complement, rather than rhetorically displace, these existing tools.

### 6.2 A Conservative Empirical Agenda

This leads to a tractable family of hypotheses rather than a final theory.

**Hypothesis 1 (Dissociation).** Some conditions should preserve much of the structural organization indexed by measures such as PCI while narrowing temporal horizon, concern breadth, persistence, or other stake-sensitive variables. Dissociative conditions are one possible test case: high-functioning individuals with high Dissociative Experiences Scale scores may exhibit near-normal structural complexity (PCI within typical range) alongside measurably reduced concern breadth (narrowed self-other inclusion, shortened temporal horizon). If structural and stake-sensitive measures always co-vary perfectly, the dissociation hypothesis fails and `d` adds nothing.

**Hypothesis 2 (Recovery timing).** If structural complexity and existential stake are not identical, then recovery from anesthesia might reveal a lag between the return of complex cortical responsiveness and the return of stable concern-sensitive organization. The key point is that the relation is empirical rather than definitional. A framework that predicts strict identity here should be distinguishable from one that predicts partial independence.

**Hypothesis 3 (Model comparison).** Across disorders of consciousness, anesthesia recovery, or concern-narrowing psychiatric conditions, models that combine a structural marker (e.g., PCI) with a stake-sensitive proxy (the composite from Table 1) should outperform models using structural markers alone, if and only if `d` tracks a real explanatory dimension. This is the simplest and fairest standard by which to evaluate the proposal.

### 6.3 Falsification Standards for `d`

These hypotheses impose clear falsification conditions. If PCI alone predicts conscious state at least as well as PCI combined with the stake-sensitive composite, then `d` adds no explanatory power and should not be retained. If PCI and the stake-sensitive composite are perfectly correlated (r > 0.9 across conditions), they are not independent constructs and the orthogonality claim is undermined. If the four proxy dimensions (Table 1) do not cohere into a reliable composite (low internal consistency, poor test-retest stability), then `d` is not a construct at all but a loose bundle of measures. At each stage, failure should count as real failure.

### 6.4 Cross-Theoretical Predictions: The Unique Contribution of Translation

The preceding hypotheses test whether `d` is a real construct. They could in principle be proposed by anyone introducing a concern-sensitive variable, without reference to a translation framework. The distinctive empirical contribution of the triadic vocabulary lies elsewhere: in predictions that emerge specifically from placing two theories' concepts into the same comparative grammar.

**Prediction 1 (Structural capacity vs. transition dynamics).** If IIT's integrated information indexes the structural capacity of the manifest organization and GWT's ignition indexes the transition event from latent to manifest, then these two features should be experimentally separable. Specifically: (a) deep sleep should preserve much of the structural capacity (relatively intact causal architecture) while suppressing transition events (no ignition); (b) epileptic seizures should produce ignition-like events (massive synchronous transitions) in systems with degraded structural capacity (poor causal differentiation). Neither IIT nor GWT alone makes a prediction about the *other* theory's measure in these conditions. The translation framework generates the prediction by mapping both concepts onto the same triadic structure and noting that they occupy different positions.

**Prediction 2 (Precision-sufficiency test for free-energy approaches).** If `d` is genuinely irreducible to precision-weighting, then two systems matched on precision profiles and expected free energy should still differ in stake-sensitive behavioral signatures---for instance, in persistence of goal-directed activity after reward withdrawal, or in the scope of costly other-regarding behavior. This prediction does not arise within the free-energy framework alone (which has no reason to expect such differences between precision-matched systems) nor from the `d` construct alone (which has no reason to specify precision as the comparison variable). It arises from the translation: placing FEP's core concepts alongside `d` in the same vocabulary reveals a specific empirical seam.

**Prediction 3 (Panpsychism's overextension boundary).** The triadic vocabulary predicts a threshold rather than a continuum for consciousness. Simple selection-like systems (thermostats, feedback controllers) should display measurable selection activity but no markers of phenomenal consciousness---no integration beyond trivial levels, no multi-scale coordination, no stake-sensitive persistence. Panpsychism predicts at least some degree of experience even in such systems. The translation framework generates the prediction by distinguishing selection activity (which it grants is ubiquitous) from phenomenal consciousness (which it claims requires additional conditions). This is testable indirectly through behavioral and neural markers: if systems with minimal structural integration and minimal concern-coupling show no markers distinguishable from non-conscious controls, the threshold claim is supported against the continuity claim.

These predictions are modest. But their value lies in their origin: they are not predictions of any single translated theory, but consequences of the comparative grammar itself. If the translation framework cannot generate at least some predictions of this kind, it is merely a filing system for existing theories. If it can, it demonstrates a form of generative capacity that goes beyond relabeling.

## 7 Limits, Failure Conditions, and Relation to the Stronger Formulation

### 7.1 Limits

The framework developed here has clear limits. It does not explain why consciousness exists in the first place. It does not provide a completed neuroscience of consciousness. It does not resolve deep metaphysical disputes about idealism, physicalism, or panpsychism. It does not yet justify strong claims about universal physical quantities or final ontological architecture. These limits are not peripheral; they are part of what makes the present reframing more defensible.

One limitation concerns scope. A translation framework can clarify disagreement without settling it. That is both its strength and its weakness. The present paper can show that some theories target different explanatory burdens, but it cannot by that fact alone tell us which burdens are most fundamental.

Another limitation concerns operationalization. The construct `d` is introduced here because existing debates seem to leave something under-described. But under-description alone is not enough to justify a new construct. The history of consciousness studies is already crowded with concepts that seem promising at the level of intuition and then become indeterminate under measurement pressure. `d` will deserve retention only if it survives that pressure.

### 7.2 Conditions of Failure

The proposal should be treated as unsuccessful under at least four conditions. First, if the triadic vocabulary fails to clarify real disputes and merely redescribes them in new jargon, its metatheoretical value is low. Second, if the `d` construct cannot be operationalized in a disciplined way, or if it adds no explanatory value beyond existing structural measures, it should not be retained. Third, if the framework repeatedly relies on claims that outstrip the available empirical and conceptual support, it reverts from a translation framework to an overextended ontology. Fourth, if the translation framework systematically underestimates genuine contradiction between theories and turns substantive incompatibility into a shallow pluralism, then it would be harmonizing the field rather than analyzing it.

These failure conditions matter because one recurrent temptation in consciousness theory is protective abstraction. When a framework is challenged, it becomes more global, more interpretive, and less vulnerable. The present paper tries to move in the opposite direction. It makes SRT weaker in order to make it more answerable.

### 7.3 Relation to the Stronger Formulation

A reader familiar with the broader SRT literature will note that the present paper operates at a deliberately lower level of commitment than the full theory (Zhang, 2026). The stronger formulation proposes that the triadic ontology describes the actual structure of reality, that the `d`-value is a substrate-independent physical quantity with three convergent formal characterizations, and that the Convergence Triad Theorem provides necessary and sufficient conditions for phenomenal consciousness. Those are substantive claims with significant empirical and philosophical consequences.

The present paper brackets those claims not because they are necessarily wrong, but because the comparative task attempted here does not require them. The weaker reading asks only whether the triadic vocabulary and the `d` construct help clarify existing debates and generate new empirical questions. If this weaker version proves useful, the stronger one gains indirect support---the comparative vocabulary would not work as well as it does if the underlying ontology were completely off track. If this weaker version fails---if the triadic distinctions prove uninformative, if `d` is empirically redundant, if the cross-theoretical predictions are not borne out---then the stronger one loses its most accessible empirical foothold. The relationship is therefore one of graded commitment: the stronger version entails the weaker, but the weaker can be evaluated independently.

## 8 Conclusion

Consciousness science may be fragmented not only because the field lacks data, but because its leading theories frequently target different explanatory burdens. This paper has argued that SRT is most promising when treated not as a completed first-order theory of consciousness, but as a candidate translation framework for relating those burdens within a shared comparative vocabulary. On this reading, the paper's contribution is not to close the debate but to reorganize it: to distinguish where theories are locally successful, where they remain in tension, and where empirical work might better discriminate among them.

The central wager of the paper is therefore modest. If the triadic vocabulary helps sort structural, access-related, adaptive, and ontological questions more cleanly, it merits provisional use. If the construct of existential stake clarifies dissociations that current debates obscure, it merits further development. If the cross-theoretical predictions generated by the translation prove empirically tractable, the framework has demonstrated more than relabeling. If none of this happens, then the framework should be narrowed further or set aside. The fate of SRT in this setting should depend less on metaphysical ambition than on whether it clarifies comparison and improves research questions.
