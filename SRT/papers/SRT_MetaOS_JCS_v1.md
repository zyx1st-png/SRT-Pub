# Selective Reality Theory as a Candidate Translation Framework for Consciousness Science

## Summary

Consciousness science has produced an unusual situation: strong local theories, weak global convergence. This paper uses Robert Lawrence Kuhn's recent taxonomy of consciousness theories as a starting point for asking whether the field's fragmentation reflects simple disagreement or partly different explanatory targets. I propose that Selective Reality Theory (SRT) is best read not as a further first-order theory of consciousness, but as a candidate translation framework for comparing those targets within a shared vocabulary. On this reading, SRT distinguishes latent conditions, manifest experiential organization, and convergent public structure, and uses this triadic distinction to reinterpret major approaches such as IIT, global workspace theory, predictive processing, panpsychism, and idealism. I then introduce a provisional construct, existential stake (`d`), and argue that it may help generate empirically tractable hypotheses when combined with existing measures such as perturbational complexity. The paper closes by specifying limits, objections, and falsification conditions for the framework.

## Abstract

Consciousness science continues to generate influential theories without approaching stable theoretical convergence. Using Robert Lawrence Kuhn's recent landscape of consciousness as a starting point, this paper argues that at least part of this proliferation reflects a structural problem: major theories often target different explanatory dimensions of consciousness rather than offering directly commensurable accounts of a single level of description. I propose that Selective Reality Theory (SRT) is most useful when read not as a further first-order theory of consciousness, but as a candidate translation framework for relating these dimensions within a shared comparative vocabulary. On this interpretation, SRT distinguishes latent conditions, manifest experiential organization, and convergent public or normative structure, and uses this triadic contrast to reinterpret recurrent tensions among IIT, global workspace theory, predictive processing/free energy approaches, panpsychism, idealism, and challenge theories. I then introduce the notion of existential stake (`d`) as a provisional construct intended to capture the depth and scope of concern-sensitive selection, and sketch several empirical hypotheses that could help test whether it adds explanatory value beyond existing structural measures. I conclude by identifying the framework's current limits and the conditions under which it should be revised or rejected.

**Keywords:** consciousness, theory comparison, metatheory, Selective Reality Theory, Integrated Information Theory, global workspace theory, predictive processing

## 1 Introduction: The Problem of Theoretical Proliferation

Consciousness science is now rich in theories but poor in convergence. This is usually treated as a sign either of premature theory formation or of deep unresolved disagreement about the nature of consciousness itself. In this paper I explore a third possibility: that at least part of the field's persistent fragmentation reflects a mismatch between explanatory targets. Some theories primarily describe access and reportability, some structural integration, some adaptive regulation, and some the metaphysical status of experience. If so, non-convergence may not simply indicate that most theories are false, but that they are often speaking past one another. Using Robert Lawrence Kuhn's recent taxonomy as a starting point, I argue that Selective Reality Theory (SRT) is most productively read as a candidate translation framework for comparing these explanatory targets, rather than as a further first-order theory competing to replace them (R. L. Kuhn, 2024; Seth and Bayne, 2022).

Kuhn's survey is especially useful because it makes visible the scale of the problem. Contemporary consciousness studies no longer presents a small number of rival grand theories, but a dense landscape of partially overlapping proposals distributed across physicalist, informational, computational, phenomenological, idealist, panpsychist, dualist, and challenge-oriented traditions (R. L. Kuhn, 2024). This plurality has often been narrated as an embarrassment for the field. Yet it may also reveal something important: theories of consciousness are frequently strongest when they are local. They perform well when answering a particular kind of question, under a particular ontology, with a particular class of evidence, but become unstable when extended beyond that domain.

Recent debate supports this diagnosis. IIT and global workspace theory remain among the best-developed frameworks in the field, yet the adversarial comparison literature has not simply produced a winner. Instead, it has repeatedly suggested that theories succeed on partly different dimensions, at different temporal and functional scales, and under different assumptions about what a successful theory ought to explain (Tononi et al., 2016; Dehaene et al., 2017; Melloni et al., 2023). Something similar holds for predictive processing and free-energy approaches, which provide unusually powerful accounts of adaptive regulation while leaving open the relation between regulation, access, and phenomenality (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013; Limanowski and Friston, 2018).

The same pattern extends beyond neuroscience-centered theories. Panpsychism, idealism, dualism, and challenge theories do not merely compete over the same causal model. They intervene at the level of metaphysical priority, explanatory adequacy, or the limits of cognition itself (Chalmers, 1995; McGinn, 1989; Strawson, 2006; Goff, 2019; Kastrup, 2019). A field containing disagreements of all these kinds should not be expected to converge through one more local experimental result alone. It needs ways of distinguishing disagreements about evidence from disagreements about scope, and disagreements about scope from disagreements about ontology.

Recent debate over whether some prominent theories count as scientific, pseudoscientific, or insufficiently testable pushes the point even further. The field is now arguing not only about which theory is best supported, but also about what standards of appraisal should govern theory choice in the first place (Negro, 2024; Gomez-Marin and Seth, 2025). That is precisely the kind of pressure under which translation-oriented and comparison-oriented work becomes useful.

The claim of this paper is therefore modest but consequential. I do not argue that SRT settles the problem of consciousness, provides a new physical foundation, or replaces existing theories. I argue instead that SRT may be useful as a comparative vocabulary for relating why major theories succeed where they do, why they fail to converge globally, and where new empirical discriminations might be found. On this reading, SRT functions as a translation framework: its value depends not on whether it terminates the debate, but on whether it clarifies the structure of disagreement and helps generate more discriminating research questions.

The paper proceeds in five steps. First, I argue that much apparent rivalry in consciousness science reflects mismatched explanatory burdens rather than simple contradiction. Second, I reformulate the core SRT distinctions in a deliberately weaker and more comparative register. Third, I introduce existential stake (`d`) as a provisional construct intended to capture concern-sensitive organization that may be under-described by existing structural frameworks. Fourth, I use this vocabulary to reread several major theory families. Finally, I outline a small set of empirical consequences that would matter if this translation framework is genuinely useful.

## 2 Why Local Success Does Not Yield Global Convergence

The persistence of theory proliferation suggests that current disagreements are not merely empirical. Integrated Information Theory, global workspace theory, predictive processing and the free energy principle, higher-order theories, and various non-physicalist approaches do not simply offer different answers to the same narrowly specified question. They often operate at different explanatory levels. IIT is strongest when describing structural irreducibility. Global workspace theory is strongest when describing broadcast, access, and report. Predictive processing and the free energy principle are strongest when describing adaptive regulation and model-based action. Panpsychism, idealism, and dualism intervene at the level of ontological priority. Challenge theories such as mysterianism diagnose the limits of explanation itself.

### 2.1 Explanatory Burden Mismatch

One reason consciousness theories fail to converge is that they are often asked to carry more burden than they were built to bear. A framework may be developed to explain one cluster of findings and then be criticized for not solving an adjacent problem that lies outside its natural scope. Global workspace theory, for example, has always been especially strong on access, report, and large-scale broadcasting dynamics (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 2017). Its critics are often right that this does not by itself explain why such broadcasting should be accompanied by experience. But that criticism does not show that the theory fails on its own home terrain; it shows that access and phenomenality cannot be assumed to be identical explanatory tasks.

IIT illustrates the complementary problem. It offers one of the most ambitious attempts to characterize the structural conditions under which a system is more or less unified from its own intrinsic perspective (Tononi et al., 2016; Albantakis et al., 2023). Yet even sympathetic readers worry that the theory's formal richness outruns its empirical tractability, and that its implications may be too liberal when detached from embodiment, function, and context. Again, this does not show that IIT has no target. It shows that a theory optimized for irreducibility and intrinsic structure may not automatically resolve questions about report, adaptive significance, or phenomenological differentiation in the same way.

Predictive processing and free-energy approaches reveal a third type of mismatch. These frameworks have unusual power because they unify perception, action, and learning around adaptive self-maintenance under uncertainty (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013). They offer a broad story about why organisms continue to exist as coherent systems. But this very breadth creates tension when they are asked to explain phenomenal character without remainder. A model of how an organism keeps itself going does not immediately become a model of what it is like for that organism to experience the world. The problem is not that adaptive regulation is irrelevant to consciousness. It is that relevance does not equal sufficiency.

### 2.2 An Example: The IIT-GWT Dispute

The ongoing IIT-GWT dispute makes the point especially clearly. In polemical form, the dispute is often framed as a direct fight over the neural basis of consciousness. In practice, however, each theory prioritizes a different explanatory axis. IIT foregrounds integrated causal structure. GWT foregrounds ignition, global availability, and flexible access. When experimental adversarial work yields mixed rather than decisive results, one possible lesson is simply that one theory is wrong. Another is that the experiment was asking two frameworks with partly different targets to compete as if they were addressing exactly the same explanatory burden (Melloni et al., 2023).

The recent large-scale COGITATE report strengthens this point rather than removing it. Its results challenged important commitments of both IIT and GNWT, while also showing that the path forward may require more systematic theory testing, shared appraisal standards, and better tools for comparing predictions across frameworks (Cogitate Consortium et al., 2025). In other words, more data did not automatically end the dispute; it clarified the need for more disciplined comparison.

This possibility does not dissolve the dispute. If a framework predicts the wrong neural signatures, it should count against it. Yet the mixed state of the literature also suggests caution. If two theories pick out partially different dimensions of the phenomenon, then even a sharp experiment may not fully arbitrate them. What we need in such cases is not diplomatic ecumenism but a better map of what exactly is being compared.

### 2.3 Incommensurability Is Real, but Not Total

There is, however, a danger in pushing this diagnosis too far. If every theory is said to target something different, then real disagreement disappears and theory choice becomes impossible. That conclusion would be too easy. Some disagreements are genuine and substantive. Physicalism and idealism are not simply different reporting languages. Panpsychism and illusionism do not merely describe adjacent aspects of the same mechanism. A translation framework therefore has to do two things at once: it must acknowledge partial incommensurability without turning all contradiction into complementarity.

This is where the more modest use of SRT begins. It does not ask us to treat all theories as equivalent. It asks whether a small comparative vocabulary can help distinguish at least three recurring moments in consciousness theories: what a theory treats as the background conditions of possible experience, what it treats as the organization of a presently manifest conscious state, and what it treats as the stable structures by which such states become reproducible, shareable, or publicly trackable. The utility of SRT depends on whether these distinctions clarify debate without erasing it.

### 2.4 Why a Translation Framework Is Worth Trying

Once these differences are seen clearly, the failure of convergence becomes less mysterious. A theory that successfully characterizes the functional organization associated with access need not settle the metaphysical status of subjectivity. A theory that offers a mathematically elegant account of integration need not explain why some integrated systems are conscious while others are not. A theory that explains adaptive self-maintenance need not thereby explain phenomenal character. In this sense, many disputes in consciousness science are not simple winner-take-all contests; they are disagreements between frameworks optimized for different explanatory burdens.

This diagnosis motivates the search for a translation framework. Such a framework should not collapse distinctions too quickly, and it should not pretend that all theories are saying the same thing. Its task is more modest: to show where apparently rival theories may be locally compatible, where their explanatory targets differ, and where genuine contradictions remain. The question, then, is whether SRT can be reformulated to serve this role.

This is not an idiosyncratic demand generated only by SRT. Recent proposals in consciousness science have already moved toward more explicit comparison frameworks, including Lakatosian models of theory appraisal and attempts to formalize how central particular measures are to different theories (Negro, 2024; Chis-Ciure et al., 2024). The present paper belongs to that family of efforts, though it proceeds through a more qualitative comparative vocabulary.

## 3 SRT as a Candidate Translation Framework

Read as a first-order metaphysics, SRT appears overly ambitious. Read as a translation framework, its central move is simpler. It distinguishes among three recurrent moments in consciousness talk: conditions that make a reality slice available, the organization of a presently manifest experiential or operational state, and the stabilization of that state into shared patterns, norms, or public structure. In the original SRT vocabulary these are rendered as `L0`, `L1`, and `L2`. For present purposes, however, their importance lies less in their metaphysical finality than in the comparative work they allow us to do.

### 3.1 The Triadic Distinction in a Weaker Register

`L0` can be treated as a label for latent possibility space: whatever background of unrealized or underdetermined structure a theory assumes when it explains why one experiential organization emerges rather than another. `L1` names the currently manifest organization that a theory takes to be directly relevant to conscious experience, access, or behavior. `L2` marks stabilized convergence: the structures, models, or shared constraints through which patterns become reproducible, publicly tractable, or normatively organized. This triadic distinction is useful because major theories of consciousness often privilege one of these moments while downplaying the others.

The important thing here is not the labels themselves. The point is to ask three comparative questions of any theory. First, what does the theory presuppose as the space of possible organization from which conscious states are drawn or selected? Second, what does it treat as the organization of a presently conscious state? Third, what stabilizes that organization across time, contexts, or observers? Many theories answer only one or two of these questions explicitly. That is not necessarily a defect. But it helps explain why they can appear more or less complete depending on what kind of explanatory demand is being made of them.

This triadic distinction also helps separate two common confusions. The first is the tendency to move too quickly from a description of manifest mechanism to a claim about ontological sufficiency. The second is the tendency to move from a claim about ontological priority to an under-described mechanism. A translation framework does not remove either problem. It simply makes the point of slippage easier to spot.

There is a useful empirical parallel here. Pérez et al. (2024) argue that neuronal markers of consciousness can be organized along partially distinct state and content dimensions, rather than treated as a single undifferentiated class of markers. Their proposal is not the same as SRT, but it illustrates the broader value of explicit conceptual dissociation. A comparative framework earns credibility when its distinctions resonate with real explanatory and measurement differences already visible in the literature.

### 3.2 How Translation Is Supposed to Work

The point of the triadic vocabulary is not to show that reality literally consists of three ontological layers. The point is to supply a compact comparative grammar. Once expressed this way, some longstanding disputes become easier to diagnose. Theories centered on neural mechanisms often privilege manifest implementation. Structural theories tend to privilege formal organization. Predictive and free-energy approaches emphasize adaptive updating and stability conditions. Panpsychist and idealist views often intervene on what should count as ontologically primary. SRT is useful insofar as it makes these differences easier to compare without prematurely collapsing them.

More concretely, translation here means three things. It means locating a theory's strongest explanatory target, making explicit what the theory leaves in the background, and clarifying whether its central concepts are being used mechanistically, structurally, phenomenologically, or metaphysically. This matters because consciousness debates are unusually vulnerable to cross-level equivocation. A term such as "integration," "access," "self-model," or "appearance" can shift its role within an argument without the shift being clearly marked.

Under this weaker reading, SRT is not an operating system in the strong sense of a master framework into which all theories must be compiled. It is closer to a translation manual that asks what kind of work each theory is doing, which distinctions it sharpens, and which distinctions it blurs. Its success therefore depends on interpretive discipline rather than totalizing reach.

This is also where the present framework aims to go beyond existing taxonomies or appraisal tools. Surveys such as Seth and Bayne (2022) are invaluable for orienting the field, and comparison tools such as measure centrality help formalize how tightly particular measures are tied to particular theories. What those approaches do not by themselves provide is a way of translating among explanatory loci. The present triadic vocabulary is intended to do more than sort theories into families or rank measures by theoretical relevance. It aims to show how one and the same dataset may bear differently on structural capacity, access execution, and stake-sensitive organization, and thereby to generate dissociation questions that are hard to formulate within any one framework alone.

### 3.3 Why This Is Not Yet a Replacement Theory

This weaker reading also imposes clear constraints. SRT should not claim that because it can redescribe another theory, it has therefore explained that theory's evidence better than the original authors. Nor should it claim that a triadic restatement automatically resolves disagreements about measurement, neural implementation, or metaphysical truth. A translation framework is useful only if it leaves room for the continued autonomy of the translated theories.

That is why the current paper intentionally avoids the strongest original SRT claims about universal ontology, completed formal proof, or final consciousness conditions. Those stronger claims may be defensible elsewhere, but they are not required for the present task. The task here is comparative clarification. If the framework cannot justify its place at that lower and more vulnerable level, there is little reason to demand acceptance at a higher one.

### 3.4 Relation to the Stronger Formulation of SRT

The stronger formulation of SRT proposes more than the present article requires. In that formulation, the triadic distinction is treated as a general ontological architecture and `d` is treated as a substrate-independent quantity with wider formal reach. The present paper brackets those claims not because they are necessarily false, but because the comparative task attempted here does not require them. This matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the current argument appropriately vulnerable: if the weaker translation-framework reading proves unhelpful, then the stronger formulation loses one of its most accessible footholds in consciousness science. Second, it clarifies that the current paper is not a strategic retreat from criticism so much as a narrowing of argumentative burden. The question here is not whether the strong formulation can ultimately be defended, but whether the weaker formulation already does useful comparative work.

## 4 Existential Stake as a Provisional Construct

The most distinctive part of the SRT proposal is the notion of existential stake, or `d`. In the stronger version of the theory, `d` is treated as a substrate-independent measure with broad formal significance. For the present paper, that stronger claim is bracketed. Here `d` should be read more cautiously as a provisional construct intended to capture something that many major theories treat only indirectly: the depth and scope of concern-sensitive selection.

### 4.1 Why Introduce `d` at All?

The intuition behind `d` is straightforward. A system may display rich structural integration, complex information processing, or sophisticated behavioral organization, yet still differ dramatically from another system in how much of the world genuinely matters to its continued organization or self-maintenance. This difference may be relevant to consciousness debates because some structural measures appear insufficient to distinguish between a merely integrated process and a process for which ongoing organization is existentially consequential. The `d` construct is an attempt to mark that gap.

The motivation for introducing `d` is not that existing theories are empty. On the contrary, many of them already contain partial analogues. In enactive and embodied approaches, concern is bound up with sense-making and the organism's coupling to its world (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). In predictive approaches, the organism's continued existence and model-guided self-maintenance are central. In everyday phenomenology, some situations matter to the subject in a way that is not captured by complexity alone. The proposal behind `d` is that these concern-sensitive dimensions may deserve a more explicit place in theory comparison.

### 4.2 What `d` Is Meant to Track

In the weaker version adopted here, `d` refers to the depth and breadth of stake-sensitive organization. Depth concerns how strongly a system's ongoing organization depends on particular outcomes, relations, or losses. Breadth concerns how many dimensions, timescales, or others are included within that field of concern. A system with narrow and shallow stake may still be highly organized. A system with broader and deeper stake may display forms of persistence, flexibility, or vulnerability that are difficult to read off from structural measures alone.

Two clarifications matter. First, `d` is not identical to intelligence. A system may solve difficult tasks while remaining narrow in what genuinely matters to its continued organization. Second, `d` is not simply equivalent to biological life. Biology may often be a powerful carrier of stake-sensitive organization, but the point of the construct is comparative, not taxonomic. What matters is whether the system's organization is tied to ongoing consequences in a way that shapes its mode of selection, persistence, and error.

### 4.3 Relation to Nearby Constructs

The most obvious neighboring construct is utility. Why not simply say that `d` is the number of things a system values? The answer is that utility, at least in its formal use, is too permissive. A utility function can be assigned to almost any optimizing description, including systems for which nothing is at issue from the system's own continued organization. `d` is intended to be narrower. It points not to abstract preference ordering alone, but to concern whose breakdown alters the system's own mode of organization, persistence, or self-maintenance.

Nor should `d` be treated as a replacement for structural complexity. The point of the construct is orthogonality, not supremacy. A system could in principle score high on some complexity measure yet remain thin in stake-sensitive organization. Another system might be relatively simpler but exhibit stronger persistence of concern under loss, disruption, or delayed reward. If such dissociations cannot be found, then `d` is either redundant or poorly specified. The construct is worth keeping only if the dissociation is real.

This is also the sense in which `d` differs from merely saying that consciousness has "importance" for the organism. Importance is too vague and easily moralized. The present proposal is colder than that. It asks whether we can identify dimensions of temporal horizon, breadth of concern, persistence under reward withdrawal, and self-other coupling that behave in disciplined ways across cases.

### 4.4 A Minimal Operational Stance

At this stage the paper does not need a maximal formal proof of `d`. It needs a disciplined proposal. The central question is whether a stake-sensitive construct adds explanatory value beyond existing structural proxies. If it does, `d` merits a place in the comparative vocabulary. If it does not, the construct should be revised or abandoned. The right standard here is not metaphysical elegance but empirical and conceptual payoff.

For that reason, I suggest a minimal operational stance rather than a definitive metric. A provisional proxy for existential stake might combine four components summarized in Table 1. None of these measures is sufficient on its own, and each is theory-laden. But together they sketch the sort of empirical profile that would make `d` more than a metaphor.

| Proxy dimension | Guiding question | Example operationalization |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Temporal horizon | How far ahead does the system continue to organize around possible outcomes? | Delay discounting slope; planning depth |
| Breadth of concern | How wide is the field of states, others, or relations treated as non-trivial? | Self-other inclusion tasks; social concern span |
| Persistence under reward loss | Does organized behavior survive short-term withdrawal of explicit reinforcement? | Goal persistence after reward removal |
| Cost sensitivity across self/other tradeoffs | How does organization change when preserving other-relevant states becomes personally costly? | Costly helping or costly coordination paradigms |

## 5 Translating Major Theory Families

On this narrower reading, SRT can be used to reinterpret several major theory families without claiming to subsume them. IIT may be read as targeting one aspect of the structure of manifest organization: the degree to which a system's organization is irreducible. Global workspace theory targets the dynamics of access, broadcasting, and reportability. Predictive processing and free-energy approaches target the adaptive maintenance of organized states under uncertainty. Higher-order theories target the relation between first-order states and meta-representational access. Panpsychist, idealist, and dualist positions intervene at the level of ontological priority or explanatory sufficiency. Challenge theories expose possible limits on any such translation.

### 5.1 IIT and Other Structural Theories

Within the present framework, IIT is read as a theory that privileges one central feature of manifest organization: intrinsic causal or informational integration (Tononi et al., 2016; Albantakis et al., 2023). In SRT terms, IIT is strongest when describing a structural aspect of `L1`, namely how unified or irreducible a present organization is from its own internal perspective. This reading does not settle whether IIT's full metaphysical interpretation is correct. It simply identifies what the theory does unusually well.

This translation has two benefits. First, it preserves IIT's explanatory strength without demanding that the theory also solve every problem of access, embodiment, and adaptive significance. Second, it clarifies why critics so often press IIT on liberal attribution. If a framework privileges intrinsic structure without an equally explicit treatment of stake, context, or embodiment, then one should expect pressure from exactly those omissions. `d` enters here not as an anti-IIT weapon, but as a candidate complement: a way of asking whether integrated organization and stake-sensitive organization are empirically dissociable.

The same point can be stated more concretely at the level of IIT's own internal architecture. The five phenomenological axioms and physical postulates of IIT are often read as carrying the theory from phenomenology to ontology in one sweep. In the present framework, they are read more cautiously as conditions on a particular type of manifest organization: existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion specify how a conscious scene must hang together if it is to count as a unified present structure. What they do not by themselves settle is whether such structure is sufficient for the full field of lived concern, temporality, and practical vulnerability that many theorists take to be bound up with consciousness. That, in turn, identifies a more specific blind spot than the generic claim that IIT is "too structural."

### 5.2 Global Workspace Theory and Access Accounts

Global workspace theory is naturally located elsewhere in the comparative map. Its core strength is not intrinsic structure but the functional profile of global availability, broadcasting, and flexible report (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 2017). In the triadic vocabulary, this makes it especially powerful at the level of manifest access organization and the transition from locally processed signals to globally available states. The framework is therefore especially well-suited to questions about report, attention, task flexibility, and the neural signatures of ignition.

Seen this way, the classical criticism of GWT can be stated more precisely. The problem is not that access is irrelevant to consciousness. The problem is that a theory of access may still underdetermine what makes a globally available state experiential rather than merely usable. The translation framework therefore preserves GWT's local success while resisting the slide from accessibility to phenomenality as if the latter simply followed automatically from the former.

### 5.3 Predictive Processing and Free-Energy Approaches

Predictive and free-energy approaches occupy a different but overlapping space in the map. Their natural strength lies in describing how organisms maintain coherence under uncertainty, minimize error, update models, and preserve themselves as adaptive systems (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013). This gives them unusually strong leverage over questions of action, perception, and self-maintenance. In the triadic vocabulary, they track the regulation of manifest organization under conditions of ongoing environmental coupling and stabilization.

Here the `d` construct has a particularly interesting relation to the existing literature. One possible outcome is that `d` turns out to add nothing beyond precision, policy selection, and expected free-energy minimization. If so, SRT should concede the point. Another possibility is that concern-sensitive organization introduces a distinction that remains blurry in current predictive accounts, including integrative proposals such as IWMT: the difference between a system that regulates successfully and a system for which the relevant couplings are existentially deep, temporally extended, and multi-dimensionally costly (Safron, 2020). The issue is not whether free-energy approaches already speak about organismic persistence. They clearly do. The issue is whether the form and scope of concern can be compared more explicitly across cases.

This yields a more precise translation claim. Free-energy approaches are powerful partly because they explain why adaptive systems cannot be treated as passive input-output devices. But the present framework suggests that two systems may be similarly proficient at prediction and control while differing in the width and persistence of the states that genuinely matter to them. If that possibility is real, then `d` is not just a vague supplement to prediction error; it is a way of asking whether successful regulation and existential organization come apart in disciplined ways.

### 5.4 Higher-Order, Enactive, and Embodied Views

Higher-order theories, enactive approaches, and embodied cognition are especially useful for showing why the map cannot remain purely structural. Higher-order theories remind us that some accounts of consciousness locate the crucial transition not in first-order representation alone but in meta-access or reflexive availability (Rosenthal, 2005). Enactive and embodied approaches insist that cognition and experience cannot be cleanly detached from the organism's practical coupling to the world (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). Both traditions therefore resist a flat treatment of conscious states as self-sufficient internal structures.

The translation framework benefits from taking these objections seriously. If SRT were merely a relabeling of internal architecture, it would fail exactly where enactive and embodied theories are strongest. The introduction of existential stake is partly intended to keep that failure in view. It is an attempt to register that conscious organization may be inseparable from the organism's field of practical concern, not only from the complexity of its internal state.

### 5.5 Panpsychism, Idealism, Dualism, and Challenge Theories

Panpsychism, idealism, and dualism enter the map at a different level. Their central intervention is not usually to specify a neural mechanism or a broadcast architecture. It is to challenge what should count as ontologically basic, explanatorily sufficient, or metaphysically coherent (Chalmers, 1995; Strawson, 2006; Goff, 2019; Kastrup, 2019). In the triadic vocabulary, these views frequently privilege what lies "beneath" or "behind" manifest organization: the conditions under which experience is even thinkable as something fundamental, distributed, or irreducible to mechanism.

This is precisely where translation becomes hardest. It would be a mistake to treat these views as merely confused forms of mechanism talk. But it would also be a mistake to treat their metaphysical force as itself a mechanism. SRT can still help here by clarifying that such theories often intervene primarily on the background conditions side of the map rather than at the level of manifest organization. That does not settle whether they are correct. It helps explain why they so often seem to talk past theories focused on report, integration, or adaptive regulation.

Panpsychism is useful here because it resists an abrupt all-or-nothing emergence story. In translation terms, it keeps pressure on any framework that would treat consciousness as appearing only after a threshold of complexity with no continuity beneath it. Its blind spot, however, is that continuity of conditions is not yet a theory of organized experiential differentiation. The combination problem reappears precisely where continuity is expected to explain structured unity.

Idealism makes a different intervention. It reminds the field that any theory of consciousness is already operating within appearance, experience, or presentation, and that a purely third-person explanatory ideal may be conceptually unstable. What it often leaves under-described is the mechanism by which stable, shareable, or lawlike organization emerges within or from that primary experiential field. In the present vocabulary, idealism strongly marks the background conditions side of the map but is often thinner on the transition to stable manifest and convergent structure.

Dualism remains valuable because it protects the intuition that what-it-is-like cannot be read off from publicly observable organization alone (Nagel, 1974; Chalmers, 1995). Its weakness, in translation terms, is not that it notices a gap, but that it often reifies the gap before explaining it. The present framework cannot dissolve the dualist challenge, but it can ask whether the challenge belongs at the level of manifest mechanism, background condition, or explanatory relation between them.

Challenge theories such as mysterianism and illusionism play another important role. They remind the field that not every gap can be closed by redescribing it. A translation framework that cannot accommodate the possibility of failure quickly becomes self-protective. For that reason, challenge theories should not be treated as external enemies of the map. They are part of the pressure that keeps the map honest.

### 5.6 What the Translation Does and Does Not Achieve

The advantage of this comparative stance is not that it resolves all disagreements. It is that it helps sort disagreements by kind. Some disagreements concern evidence. Some concern explanatory scope. Some concern what a theory should be expected to explain at all. A translation framework is useful if it helps prevent category mistakes such as treating a theory of access as though it were already a theory of phenomenality, or treating a theory of ontological priority as though it had already supplied a mechanism.

This also clarifies what SRT should not claim. It should not claim that all theories are secretly the same, that every disagreement is merely verbal, or that one triadic vocabulary abolishes the need for theory choice. Instead, it should claim only that some of the field's fragmentation becomes more intelligible when we distinguish structural, access-related, adaptive, and ontological explanatory targets more carefully.

This perspective is also relevant to current debates about machine consciousness. Recent work has argued that assessments of AI consciousness should proceed by deriving indicators from existing theories rather than by relying on a single behavioral litmus test or a simple Turing-style proxy (Butlin et al., 2025). Whether or not one agrees with those proposals, they support the broader methodological point that theory-guided indicator mapping is becoming increasingly central to the field.

The main comparative payoff can be summarized compactly. Table 2 does not claim to settle any theory. It records the more modest point that different theory families appear strongest when they are treated as targeting different loci in the explanatory space, and that the present framework may therefore help diagnose not only where a theory succeeds, but where it predictably becomes overextended.

## 6 Empirical Payoff

A metatheoretical paper justifies itself only if it sharpens empirical questions rather than floating above them. The most promising empirical consequence of the present framework concerns the relation between structural measures of complexity and stake-sensitive measures of concern, persistence, and self-relevant organization. If a construct like `d` adds anything, it should do so by identifying cases in which structural complexity and concern-sensitive organization come apart.

### 6.1 Why PCI Is the Right Starting Point

One plausible test domain is the relation between perturbational complexity and conscious state. Existing work on measures such as PCI has provided one of the most important cross-condition tools in consciousness science because it gives a way of tracking complexity while reducing dependence on overt report (Casali et al., 2013; Massimini and Tononi, 2018). A translation-framework reading of SRT suggests that this structural dimension may be powerful but incomplete. If so, one would expect some conditions in which structural complexity is relatively preserved while concern-sensitive organization narrows, and other conditions in which concern-sensitive organization recovers on a different time course from structural complexity.

The value of starting here is methodological. If `d` cannot improve on, complement, or clarify established structural markers, it should not be introduced. The burden is therefore squarely on the SRT proposal. It needs to show that a stake-sensitive dimension can be operationalized without becoming either a disguised moral vocabulary or a noisy bundle of behavioral variables.

Recent work supports both the promise and the caution required here. Pérez et al. (2024) show that putative neuronal markers can sort differently along state and content dimensions, which already warns against flattening all markers into a single explanatory axis. At the same time, Xu et al. (2024) report that PCIst tracked responsiveness and prognosis in a randomized controlled rTMS study of minimally conscious patients, reinforcing the practical importance of structural complexity measures in disorders-of-consciousness research. Any proposed stake-sensitive construct must therefore complement, rather than rhetorically displace, these existing tools.

### 6.2 A Conservative Empirical Agenda

This leads to a tractable family of hypotheses rather than a final theory. Recovery from anesthesia, dissociative conditions, and some disorders of consciousness may provide better test beds than highly speculative cross-scale analogies. The key question is whether adding a disciplined proxy for existential stake improves prediction, explanation, or discrimination beyond existing measures. If it does not, the proposal loses force.

The first hypothesis is a dissociation hypothesis: some conditions should preserve much of the structural organization indexed by measures such as PCI while narrowing temporal horizon, concern breadth, persistence, or other stake-sensitive variables. Dissociative conditions are one possible test case, not because they straightforwardly validate SRT, but because they may reveal a gap between structural organization and concern organization.

The second hypothesis is a recovery-timing hypothesis. If structural complexity and existential stake are not identical, then recovery from anesthesia might reveal a lag between the return of complex cortical responsiveness and the return of stable concern-sensitive organization. The key point is not that one must always come before the other; the key point is that the relation is empirical rather than definitional. A framework that predicts strict identity here should be distinguishable from one that predicts partial independence.

The third hypothesis is a model-comparison hypothesis. Across disorders of consciousness, anesthesia recovery, or concern-narrowing psychiatric conditions, models that combine a structural marker with a stake-sensitive proxy should outperform models using structural markers alone, if and only if `d` tracks a real explanatory dimension. This is the simplest and fairest standard by which to evaluate the proposal.

### 6.3 A Cross-Theoretical Prediction

The preceding hypotheses are intentionally conservative, but the translation framework also permits at least one stronger prediction that is difficult to formulate from any single theory alone. If IIT-like measures track structural capacity, GWT-like measures track access execution, and `d`-like measures track stake-sensitive organization, then these three dimensions should be only partially coupled rather than strictly coextensive. Put differently, the framework predicts a family of three-way dissociations rather than a single master axis of consciousness.

This yields a more specific experimental expectation. There should be states in which structural capacity remains relatively preserved while access organization is weak or absent; states in which access-like global recruitment occurs without the richer differentiated structure that would support stable experience; and states in which both structure and access are partly restored while stake-sensitive organization remains narrowed. Deep sleep, seizure-like states, anesthesia emergence, dissociative conditions, and some disorders of consciousness may instantiate different parts of this space. No one of these examples is decisive on its own. The prediction is comparative: datasets should be better explained by partially separable latent dimensions of structure, access, and stake than by a single monotonic variable.

That is a distinctive payoff of the present framework. IIT alone does not predict how access-like execution and stake-sensitive organization should dissociate from structural capacity. GWT alone does not predict how access-related ignition should come apart from intrinsic structure or concern organization. A stake-only proposal would not by itself generate a structured relation to existing theories. The cross-theoretical prediction arises only once the three explanatory loci are placed in the same comparative space.

### 6.4 A Minimal Proxy Strategy

No single behavioral measure should be treated as "the" measure of existential stake. But a disciplined composite may still be possible. Four candidate dimensions seem especially relevant: temporal horizon, breadth of social or practical concern, persistence of organization after reward withdrawal, and sensitivity to costs imposed on self versus others. None of these should be romanticized. Each can be distorted by pathology, training, species differences, and task design. The point is not to find purity, but to find whether a reproducible cluster exists.

A useful empirical strategy would therefore be incremental. First, establish whether the stake-sensitive variables correlate strongly enough with one another to form a meaningful construct at all. Second, test whether that construct is separable from existing complexity measures. Third, evaluate whether the combined model improves prediction across cases. At each stage, failure should count as real failure, not as evidence that the construct has become deeper and more elusive.

### 6.5 Why This Narrow Agenda Is Better Than a Grand One

The original SRT framework makes more expansive empirical suggestions, including cross-scale and cross-domain analogies. For a JCS-style comparative paper, that ambition should be resisted. The most persuasive empirical contribution of the present article will not be a claim that one vocabulary unifies quantum, neural, social, and cosmic selection dynamics. It will be a more modest claim: that consciousness science may benefit from distinguishing structural complexity from stake-sensitive organization, and that this distinction may be testable in already established research domains.

## 7 Limits and Conditions of Failure

The framework developed here has clear limits. It does not explain why consciousness exists in the first place. It does not provide a completed neuroscience of consciousness. It does not resolve deep metaphysical disputes about idealism, physicalism, or panpsychism. It does not yet justify strong claims about universal physical quantities or final ontological architecture. These limits are not peripheral; they are part of what makes the present reframing more defensible.

One limitation concerns scope. A translation framework can clarify disagreement without settling it. That is both its strength and its weakness. The present paper can show that some theories target different explanatory burdens, but it cannot by that fact alone tell us which burdens are most fundamental. There remains an irreducibly substantive question about whether access, integration, regulation, reflexivity, or ontological priority should be treated as primary.

Another limitation concerns operationalization. The construct `d` is introduced here because existing debates seem to leave something under-described. But under-description alone is not enough to justify a new construct. The history of consciousness studies is already crowded with concepts that seem promising at the level of intuition and then become indeterminate under measurement pressure. `d` will deserve retention only if it survives that pressure.

The proposal should therefore be treated as unsuccessful under at least four conditions. First, if the triadic vocabulary fails to clarify real disputes and merely redescribes them in new jargon, its metatheoretical value is low. Second, if the `d` construct cannot be operationalized in a disciplined way, or if it adds no explanatory value beyond existing structural measures, it should not be retained. Third, if the framework repeatedly relies on claims that outstrip the available empirical and conceptual support, it reverts from a translation framework to an overextended ontology. Fourth, if the translation framework systematically underestimates genuine contradiction between theories and turns substantive incompatibility into a shallow pluralism, then it would be harmonizing the field rather than analyzing it.

These failure conditions matter because one recurrent temptation in consciousness theory is protective abstraction. When a framework is challenged, it becomes more global, more interpretive, and less vulnerable. The present paper tries to move in the opposite direction. It makes SRT weaker in order to make it more answerable. If that weaker version still proves unhelpful, then the problem should be acknowledged directly.

## 8 Conclusion

Consciousness science may be fragmented not only because the field lacks data, but because its leading theories frequently target different explanatory burdens. This paper has argued that SRT is most promising when treated not as a completed first-order theory of consciousness, but as a candidate translation framework for relating those burdens within a shared comparative vocabulary. On this reading, the paper's contribution is not to close the debate but to reorganize it: to distinguish where theories are locally successful, where they remain in tension, and where empirical work might better discriminate among them.

The central wager of the paper is therefore modest. If the triadic vocabulary helps sort structural, access-related, adaptive, and ontological questions more cleanly, it merits provisional use. If the construct of existential stake clarifies dissociations that current debates obscure, it merits further development. If neither happens, then the framework should be narrowed further or set aside. The fate of SRT in this setting should depend less on metaphysical ambition than on whether it clarifies comparison and improves research questions.
