# GRG Civilizational Learning Architecture v0.1

## 0. Question

How can accumulated social and civilizational experience become a disciplined input to GRG without turning history into anecdote or morality into persistence?

## 1. Civilization as distributed learning

Working programme hypothesis:

~~~text
civilization
= many finite positions
  externalizing experience,
  preserving partial regularities,
  creating institutions,
  intervening on future conditions,
  receiving consequences,
  and revising inherited expectations.
~~~

This is a research perspective, not a claim that civilization is one subject or one Bearer.

## 2. Civilizational memory surfaces

Relevant memory is distributed across:

- scientific literature;
- law;
- administrative records;
- markets and prices;
- firms and organizations;
- infrastructure;
- technologies;
- education;
- customs;
- moral traditions;
- religion;
- philosophy;
- art;
- oral and written history;
- family / community practice;
- ecological landscapes;
- failures and disasters.

These surfaces have different biases and survival processes.

What survives in an archive is not automatically what mattered most.

## 3. Civilizational Experience Record

Each case should record:

~~~text
case =
period =
geography / institutional setting =
positions / participants =
initial generative relation =
rule / institution / technology =
intervention / shock =
expected continuation =
actual short-term consequence =
actual long-term consequence =
distribution of consequence =
externalized cost =
adaptation / resistance =
relation strengthened =
relation weakened =
relation substituted / hollowed =
evidence sources =
counterevidence =
alternative explanation =
~~~

## 4. Evidence discipline

Civilizational evidence should separate:

~~~text
event existence
causal attribution
mechanism
distributional consequence
long-run consequence
normative interpretation
~~~

A case may have strong evidence for the event and weak evidence for causality.

Historical recurrence may nominate a GRG candidate.

It does not prove universal law.

## 5. Institutional reading

An institution may be analyzed as:

~~~text
externalized generative expectation
+
enforcement / incentive structure
+
memory / classification system
+
allocation of consequence
~~~

Questions:

- what behavior / relation does it make easier?
- what does it make costly or inaccessible?
- what history does it preserve?
- whose position is encoded?
- who receives consequences?
- what costs are exported outside its accounting boundary?
- can participants revise it?
- does it preserve its own operation by destroying participant generativity?

Institutional stability is therefore not a sufficient positive normative result.

## 6. Social relation analysis

For social systems, GRG should distinguish:

~~~text
coordination
cooperation
dependency
domination
extraction
reciprocity
care
competition
collective action
institutional maintenance
~~~

These cannot be collapsed into generic "relation".

For each, the causal and normative burdens differ.

## 7. Generative health without moral shortcut

Candidate descriptive questions:

- does the relation reproduce its enabling conditions?
- does it require hidden depletion?
- does it export irreversible cost?
- does it maintain participant agency?
- can it adapt without destroying its participants?
- does it monopolize future selectability?
- does it produce fragile dependence?
- does it increase or reduce correction capacity?

These questions can support indexed G-stronger / G-weaker analysis.

They do not by themselves settle justice, rights or moral legitimacy.

## 8. Multi-position normativity

Civilizational analysis must permit:

~~~text
relation beneficial from position A
and harmful from position B
~~~

Therefore every normative record should state:

- whose expectation;
- whose continuation;
- whose cost;
- which scale;
- which horizon.

A whole-level gain cannot silently cancel participant-level destruction.

Participant interest cannot automatically veto every higher-order coordination structure.

Both require explicit burdens.

## 9. Civilization-level conflicts

Important research families include:

### C1 — local success / global depletion

A relation works locally by exporting costs to ecological, temporal or social background.

### C2 — short-term efficiency / long-term option loss

Optimization narrows future selectability.

### C3 — institutional persistence / participant generativity

An institution survives by reducing participants' capacity to revise it.

### C4 — coordination / domination ambiguity

Stable coordination may encode asymmetrical consequence-bearing.

### C5 — innovation / continuity tension

Novelty increases option space while destroying valuable accumulated organization.

### C6 — knowledge / power coupling

Objectification and classification improve coordination but also control what becomes visible or governable.

### C7 — technological mediation

Technology changes not only choices but the field of future choices.

These are programme problem families, not established universal laws.

## 10. Comparative civilizational learning

Do not ask only:

~~~text
Which society / institution won?
~~~

Ask:

~~~text
What relation was instantiated?
Under what constraints?
What consequences appeared at which horizons?
What hidden support was required?
What revision mechanisms existed?
What failed when conditions changed?
~~~

Comparisons should preserve:

- path dependence;
- geography;
- technology;
- power;
- ecology;
- demography;
- measurement differences.

## 11. Scientific knowledge inside civilization

Science itself is part of the civilizational learning loop.

It provides unusually strong procedures for:

- explicit objectification;
- replication;
- causal testing;
- formal constraint;
- public error correction.

But scientific objectification remains finite.

GRG should neither demote science to "one narrative" nor turn current scientific objects into the final ontology floor.

## 12. AI and civilizational memory

AI can make a new scale of cross-record comparison possible.

Potential role:

~~~text
retrieve
-> normalize
-> link
-> compare
-> generate candidate relation
-> find counterexample
-> update atlas
~~~

Risks:

- archive bias amplification;
- false equivalence;
- dominant-language bias;
- evidence flattening;
- hallucinated convergence;
- hidden normative assumptions;
- over-centralized epistemic authority.

AI must remain auditable and corrigible.

## 13. Outputs

Initial civilizational programme outputs should be:

1. a case-record schema;
2. a domain/civilization atlas;
3. a relation library;
4. a cross-case comparison matrix;
5. a generative-expectation ledger;
6. a conflict / multi-position ledger;
7. calibration cases where the framework is likely to fail.

## 14. First implementation rule

Do not begin by trying to encode all of world history.

Seed the architecture with a small diverse set:

- one scientific/technical case;
- one biological/ecological case;
- one market/institution case;
- one governance/law case;
- one community/social case;
- one long-duration civilizational case.

Choose cases with reasonably strong source access and explicit failure pressure.

The purpose of the first set is to test the schema, not to prove a theory of civilization.

## 15. Canonical and political guard

This architecture is a research programme.

It is not:

- a political ideology;
- an endorsement of a party / regime;
- a policy-ranking engine;
- a moral scoring system;
- a claim of civilizational superiority.

Policy or institutional recommendations require separate evidence and explicit normative premises.
