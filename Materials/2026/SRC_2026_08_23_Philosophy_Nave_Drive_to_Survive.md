# SourceCard — Kathryn Nave, *A Drive to Survive*

## 1. One-line summary

Kathryn Nave argues that the Free Energy Principle is useful as an approximate modeling framework for many homeostatic and predictive-control dynamics, but fails as a first principle of life because stability, steady-state dynamics and Markov-blanket formalisms neither individuate living systems nor ground biological normativity; she develops a processual, constraint-closure account of biological autonomy in which precarious constraints are regenerated by the activity they enable.

## 3. Core claims of the source

### 3.1 Control is a means, not the existential goal

The Introduction contrasts engineered control systems with living cells. Homeostatic/predictive control can help an organism persist, but the fact that control succeeds does not itself explain why any state matters to the system. The central target is therefore not control alone but the organism's precarious dependence on its own activity.

**Anchor**: Introduction, book pp. 1–10 (PDF pp. 12–21).

### 3.2 Predictive processing does not generate its own normativity

Chapters 1–3 distinguish minimal predictive covariation from stronger inferential/representational claims and argue that prediction error, model adequacy, learning and action require an independently justified norm if they are to be read as success/failure rather than mere covariation.

**Anchors**: Ch. 2, book pp. 39–54 (PDF pp. 50–65); Ch. 3, especially §§3.4–3.6, book pp. 73–84 (PDF pp. 84–95).

### 3.3 Stability is too general for agency and too restrictive for life

Chapters 4, 8 and 9 argue that stability, steady states, recurrence and fixed dynamical organization are shared by many nonliving systems while living systems characteristically undergo path-dependent development, material turnover, organizational transformation and open-ended changes in effective possibility space.

**Anchors**: Ch. 4, book pp. 85–102 (PDF pp. 96–113); Ch. 8, book pp. 157–178 (PDF pp. 168–189); Ch. 9, book pp. 179–210 (PDF pp. 190–221).

### 3.4 Markov blankets do not by themselves individuate organisms

Chapters 5–6 and the Appendix distinguish the statistical Markov blanket from stronger causal/ontological readings. A blanket depends on prior choices about variables, grain and units; even a realist causal-graph interpretation requires additional metaphysical premises and does not itself tell us what the world's ultimate or organism-relevant units are.

**Anchors**: Ch. 5, book pp. 103–122 (PDF pp. 114–133); Ch. 6, book pp. 123–138 (PDF pp. 134–149); Appendix, especially A.3–A.4, book pp. 266–270 (PDF pp. 277–281).

### 3.5 Process closure is too permissive; constraint closure targets the relevant causal level

Chapters 7 and 10 distinguish generic cyclic/process closure from biological autonomy. A machine-like process network can be mutually dependent while relying on stable structural constraints supplied as unexplained background. Constraint closure instead asks whether each relevant constraint both enables processes and depends, directly or through the network, on processes made possible by other constraints in the same organization.

**Anchors**: Ch. 7, book pp. 139–156 (PDF pp. 150–167); Ch. 10 §§10.1–10.3, book pp. 218–240 (PDF pp. 229–251).

### 3.6 Structure / function can be timescale-relative

In the constraint-closure discussion, an enzyme may be relatively invariant with respect to a fast reaction yet degrade and require regeneration over a slower timescale. Nave uses this to motivate a processual reading in which biological constraints are not intrinsically stable things standing outside activity but extrinsically stabilized processes.

**Anchor**: Ch. 10 §10.2.2, book pp. 226–230 (PDF pp. 237–241).

### 3.7 Biological normativity is proposed as binding but violable

Chapter 11 argues that a norm differs from a deterministic law because it can genuinely be violated. In Nave's account, biological norms gain objective force because a constraint-closed organism must keep doing regenerative work to continue existing, yet can fail and cease to exist. Particular norms may change with organization and environment without thereby becoming merely subjective.

**Anchor**: Ch. 11, especially §§11.1–11.2, book pp. 243–252 (PDF pp. 254–263).

### 3.8 FEP is retained as an approximation / strategy, not a first principle of life

Chapter 12 concludes that predictive processing and free-energy models may implement useful control, anticipation and offline simulation once a teleological context is already supplied. Stability remains common because preserving a historically successful organization is often a conservative strategy under uncertainty, not because stability itself defines life.

**Anchor**: Ch. 12, book pp. 253–258 (PDF pp. 264–269).

## 4. Evidence / method

This is primarily a philosophical monograph synthesizing philosophy of biology, enactivism, cybernetics, theoretical biology, dynamical systems, predictive processing and FEP literature. Its main contribution is conceptual and argumentative rather than a new empirical dataset.

Nave's positive account relies heavily on existing work by Maturana and Varela, Di Paolo, Thompson, Moreno, Mossio, Montévil, Longo, Kauffman, Rosen, Jonas and related processual/organizational biology. Empirical examples (e.g. bacterial metabolic switching, development, biological turnover) support bounded biological claims but do not by themselves establish the book's full metaphysical conclusions.

## 5. Limits

1. The book's strongest positive conclusion is biologically centered: metabolic/constraint-closed self-production is treated as the basis of biological normativity and genuine intentionality.
2. That does not establish that metabolism is necessary for every possible SRT bearer, digital system, institution or dyadic process.
3. The book does not prove SRT's L0, `epsilon_pg`, `d`, `Psi_f`, Stable ISP or subjecthood claims.
4. The critique of Markov-blanket realism does not prove that all boundaries are observer-created or that objective individuation is impossible.
5. Processual identity and open-ended biological change support a strong bridge to history-dependent possibility-space rewriting, but do not make generative reselectability a P1 requirement for Selection or every Stable ISP.
6. Nave's contrast between organisms and machines must not be imported as `machine -> no Selection`; current SRT governance explicitly forbids that inference.
