# SourceCard: Hirschhorn, Negro & Mudrik — *The perceived role of consciousness in moral status attributions*

## 1. One-line summary

Across a main survey (`N=1143`, split into exploratory `N=350` and preregistered confirmatory `N=793`) plus a preregistered follow-up (`N=350`), consciousness attribution was strongly associated with moral-status attribution but dissociated in both directions; moral decisions also varied with valence, self-awareness, thinking, target character/relationship and the attributor's background. For SRT, the source is most useful as pressure to keep **consciousness / subjecthood / actual moral standing / moral-status attribution / governance protection** typed apart.

## 2. Source boundary and evidence anchors

This card is grounded in the user-supplied full PDF. The manuscript title page names Rony Hirschhorn, Niccolò Negro and Liad Mudrik and presents the study as an empirical investigation of public moral-status attributions.

Load-bearing anchors:

- **pp. 1–3, Abstract + Introduction + Study Overview:** main sample size, preregistered confirmatory split, follow-up, 24 target entities, direct questions, attribution questions and moral dilemmas.
- **pp. 4–7, Fig. 1 + necessity/sufficiency section:** strong consciousness–moral-status association plus two-way dissociations; tree / LLM / fetus above the diagonal, rat / mosquito / fruit-fly below; follow-up replication and explicit moral-status-vs-significance clarification.
- **pp. 7–9, KPT dilemma:** separate contributions of consciousness, valence and intentions/goals; valence shows the strongest single protective association against killing in the reported model.
- **pp. 9–10, Fig. 3:** self-awareness, pleasure/suffering and thinking are selected more often than the Nagelian “something it is like” formulation as important for moral consideration.

## 3. Core source claims

### 3.1 Consciousness matters, but does not exhaust moral-status attribution

The source reports a strong positive association between consciousness and moral-status ratings across entities and participants. It also reports robust off-diagonal cases:

```text
moral-status attribution > consciousness attribution:
  tree
  large language model
  24-week human fetus

consciousness attribution > moral-status attribution:
  rat
  mosquito
  fruit-fly / bat depending on sample
```

The follow-up explicitly distinguishes moral status, defined in terms of the entity's own intrinsic interests, from merely instrumental moral significance and reports the same broad dissociation pattern.

Source-level conclusion:

```text
consciousness is strongly associated with attributed moral status
but is not perceived as strictly necessary or strictly sufficient
```

This is a result about **human judgments / attributions**. It is not an empirical demonstration of what actually has moral status.

### 3.2 Moral judgment is multi-criterion

In the Kill-to-Pass-a-Test dilemma, all three manipulated properties reduce willingness to kill when present alone, with the reported ordering:

```text
valence > consciousness > intentions/goals
```

for protective association in the fitted model. Combinations of features are additionally protective in some comparisons.

In the direct feature question, the most frequently selected features include:

```text
self-awareness
feelings of pleasure and suffering
thinking
```

while the “something it is like to be that creature/system” formulation ranks much lower.

The source therefore does not support a single-property account of ordinary moral-status judgment.

### 3.3 Target character and relation matter

In Earth-in-Danger choices, participants strongly favor a random person over a dog but reverse much of that preference when the person is described as a dictator responsible for millions of deaths. Participants also favor their own pet more than a random dog, especially among actual pet owners.

The source treats harmfulness / moral character and relational closeness as non-mental factors associated with moral choices.

### 3.4 The attributor matters

Participant choices cluster into distinct profiles. Self-reported experience with animals, AI, ethics and consciousness research predicts cluster membership above chance. Higher reported experience with AI is associated with higher consciousness and moral-status attributions to artificial systems; animal experience is associated with higher moral-status attributions to most non-human animals.

Safe source-level compression:

```text
moral-status attribution is not a function of target properties alone
```

The manuscript discusses a relational interpretation but explicitly states that the data do not establish a particular metaethical position.

## 4. Limits that must travel with the result

1. **Consciousness was intentionally left undefined.** Participants were instructed to use their own understanding, because the field lacks an agreed definition. The authors report no systematic moral-status differences across a coarse grouping of consciousness conceptions, but the construct remains heterogeneous.
2. **Moral status is a philosophically loaded survey concept.** The follow-up reduces, but cannot eliminate, possible conflation with moral significance.
3. **Dilemmas are hypothetical and life/death framing adds confounds.** The authors use triangulation to reduce dependence on any one probe.
4. **Cultural coverage and expertise measurement are limited.** The sample is large/international but not culturally exhaustive; expertise is self-reported.
5. **Choice data do not reveal the full generative reasoning process.** The source shows patterns of judgment, not an ontological mechanism of moral standing.
