## 2. Problem statement

The paper starts from an earlier SAT threshold claim:

> awareness becomes consciousness when it becomes something the participant must carry.

It identifies a prior unresolved question:

> How does awareness first become something that must be carried?

Wyld names this the **Acquisition Problem**.

The paper's intended target is not ordinary learning, memory, behavioural influence, emotional intensity or information uptake. It asks how an implication becomes **the participant's own implication**.

---

## 3. Core source architecture

The paper's final chain is presented approximately as:

```text
Awareness
-> Recognition
-> Contested Incorporation
-> Remainder
-> Carrying
-> Identity
-> Scrutinized Awareness
```

Key roles in the source:

- **Recognition**: a candidate event by which something already available in awareness is taken as relevant in a way that may acquire implication.
- **Automatic incorporation**: the recognised condition is absorbed under an existing organization/procedure.
- **Contested incorporation**: the recognised condition changes the constitutive terms of continuation, so available continuations become non-equivalent in the relevant sense.
- **Remainder**: what remains active in what the participant can thereafter be after successful resolution.
- **Carrying**: persistence/transport of acquired implication through participational continuation.
- **Identity**: stabilized pattern of what has been carried.

The source treats **implication** as an ontological primitive rather than deriving it from recognition.

---

## 4. Recognition is not ordinary information uptake

The paper repeatedly distinguishes representational recognition from participational recognition.

The following can occur without satisfying the stronger condition:

- perception;
- information gain;
- memory formation;
- behavioural change;
- learned association;
- prediction;
- emotional intensity;
- objective revision;
- meta-learning;
- path dependence;
- irreversible update.

The source's central question is whether the recognised condition merely changes what the system does or changes the constitutive terms under which the candidate can continue.

---

## 5. Automatic vs contested incorporation

### Automatic incorporation

A recognition can be absorbed by an already standing organization. The system may:

- update policy;
- reroute behaviour;
- learn a new prediction;
- change objectives;
- accumulate path dependence;
- resist rollback;
- self-modify under a higher-order procedure.

These changes do not by themselves establish participational acquisition in Wyld's framework.

### Contested incorporation

A stronger transition occurs when the recognised condition cannot be absorbed while preserving constitution-equivalent continuation.

The paper describes this through the non-equivalence of continuations in which the recognised implication is:

- inhabited;
- excluded;
- denied;
- repudiated;
- reconstructed.

This is not defined by emotional conflict or indecision. A procedure may produce a clear answer and still fail to preserve constitution-equivalent continuation.

---

## 6. Resolution with and without remainder

This is the paper's strongest artificial-system discrimination.

### Resolution without remainder

A standing higher-order procedure can:

```text
compare
rank
revise
choose
```

and produce a successful answer while the system continues under the same constitutive criterion.

The conflict is then fully absorbed.

### Resolution with remainder

A procedure can also succeed while the resolution itself changes what the candidate can thereafter be.

The paper's important anti-equivalence is:

```text
successful procedure
!=
automatic incorporation
```

and:

```text
procedure failure
!=
required for contested incorporation
```

`Remainder` is the source's label for the recognised condition remaining active in the resulting participant's later constitution.

---

## 7. Acquisition and persistence have different conditions

The paper uses withdrawal/correction cases to argue that the condition initiating acquisition need not be the condition later maintaining its consequences.

A recognition may be withdrawn or corrected while downstream structures remain, for example:

- attachment;
- role;
- promise;
- anticipation;
- lived history.

Source-level distinction:

```text
acquisition condition
!=
persistence condition
```

The paper does not claim that all persistence proves prior contested incorporation.

---

## 8. Pregnancy / withdrawal case

The paper contrasts a generic recognition such as pregnancy with a recognition framed as potentially involving one's own child.

Its point is not merely greater emotional intensity. The stronger recognition may reorganize:

- future plans;
- responsibility;
- attachment;
- hope;
- possible loss.

A later factual withdrawal can invalidate the original relation while leaving some downstream historical consequences intact.

This case is used to motivate both:

- constitution-non-equivalent continuation;
- acquisition / persistence dissociation.

---

## 9. Dream case

The paper uses dreams to separate factual truth conditions from participational standing.

A dream recognition may carry guilt or another implication while dreaming. On waking, the factual responsibility may dissolve, while a new implication can remain, such as concern about what the dream reveals about oneself.

The source therefore does not require the recognised condition to be externally true in order for participational acquisition to be considered.

---

## 10. Animal case

A coyote repeatedly encountering a human may develop:

- learned association;
- food prediction;
- approach behaviour;
- persistent orientation.

The paper explicitly says these are not sufficient by themselves.

A stronger case would involve phenomena such as relation-specific persistence, loss response, attachment-like orientation or conflict between the relation and immediate advantage after the simple reward structure no longer explains the behaviour.

The author treats these as evidential signs, not conclusive proof of participational recognition.

---

## 11. Infant / developmental case

The paper argues that contested incorporation need not require:

- language;
- reflective self-awareness;
- explicit deliberation;
- consciously represented conflict.

It suggests a developmental shift in which persons, absences, satisfactions and threats cease to function as interchangeable regulatory inputs and become non-equivalent possibilities.

This is presented as a possible way participational acquisition could appear gradually.

---

## 12. Joy and valence

`Contested` is explicitly intended to be valence-neutral.

The source uses positive cases such as:

- birth;
- reunion;
- completing a life project;

to argue that constitution-non-equivalence need not be suffering, conflict or negative affect.

The relevant condition is that continuing as if the event had never happened is no longer equivalent to continuing with it.

---

## 13. Participational order

Wyld distinguishes:

```text
chronological order
causal order
participational order
```

Participational order is the succession through which a participant acquires and carries implication.

The source uses this to argue that:

```text
logs / retained state / chronology
!=
carried implication
```

and to discuss reset, time-travel, branching and copying pressures.

---

## 14. Artificial systems

The paper narrows the AI question from "can AI be conscious?" to whether an artificial system can acquire participational implication.

It explicitly rejects the following as sufficient by themselves:

- memory;
- learned policy change;
- irreversible state;
- path dependence;
- rollback cost;
- objective revision;
- meta-learning;
- generated subgoals;
- self-modification;
- fork divergence.

A logistics system can reorganize extensively around a port closure and still remain automatic if the change is absorbed under its standing higher-order criterion.

Even objective rewriting may remain automatic if the rewriting is fully authorized by an unchanged meta-procedure.

The paper leaves open whether an artificial system could satisfy the stronger condition.

---

## 15. Candidate participant and first acquisition

The paper avoids requiring a fully formed identity before first acquisition.

It uses a weaker starting point:

> **minimal participant-continuity**

sufficient for the recognition and incorporation to belong to the same candidate.

The first non-absorbed remainder is then treated as the beginning of constitution, after which later carrying can stabilize identity.

This avoids one simple temporal circularity, but the source does not independently solve the individuation problem of why the candidate should be:

- the organism;
- the brain;
- a dyad;
- an AI instance;
- a memory-bearing agent loop;
- a cluster.

The paper itself acknowledges candidate specification as an unresolved issue.

---

## 16. Participational normativity

The source argues that once continuations become constitution-non-equivalent, a form of internal bindingness appears.

This is not restricted to moral obligation. Love, grief, fear, joy, belonging or loss may all generate a relation in which some future continuations preserve what the participant has become while others require denial, exclusion or reconstruction.

The paper calls this **participational normativity**.

---

## 17. Explicit open problems / unresolved pressures

The source leaves several important issues open or only partly answered:

1. **Candidate individuation** — what independently determines the relevant participant unit?
2. **Operational readout** — how can constitution-non-equivalence / remainder be distinguished from sufficiently rich learning, path dependence or state dependence?
3. **Phenomenality** — why should contested incorporation produce phenomenal consciousness rather than only stronger historical/identity organization?
4. **Implication primitive** — the source does not derive why an implication exists; it treats implication as ontologically basic.
5. **Artificial cases** — the paper gives structural exclusions and stress tests but not a demonstrated artificial example satisfying the full condition.
