# SourceCard: Berio & Musholt, *Seeing What To Do: Norms, Scripts, and Social Affordances*

## 1. One-line summary

Berio and Musholt argue that social norms shape interpersonal affordance perception through scripts: norm-laden, temporally ordered and socially distributed event-templates that can be learned implicitly or explicitly, filter which actions become salient for role-situated agents, and mediate bidirectionally between tacit skill and explicit norm articulation or disruption.

## 2. Source and bibliographic boundary

The supplied PDF identifies:

```text
Leda Berio, University College Dublin
Kristina Musholt, Leipzig University
Seeing What To Do: Norms, Scripts, and Social Affordances
pre-print; forthcoming in Philosophers' Imprint
```

The supplied preprint does not establish a publication date or DOI. This SourceCard therefore records the venue as forthcoming and does not infer missing metadata.

The paper is a philosophy-of-cognition argument and synthesis. It discusses empirical work on affordance modulation, ownership, semantic processing and implicit bias, but it does not itself report a new experimental dataset or settle the low-level neural / visual mechanism by which scripts influence affordance perception.

## 3. Author's argumentative route

The paper's route can be compressed as:

```text
social norms shape what actions feel possible / appropriate
-> interpersonal affordances occur within larger social action sequences
-> purely ecological / habit accounts capture implicit learning but underdescribe semantic and explicit-norm effects
-> purely explicit representational accounts overstate conscious rule representation
-> scripts provide an implicit / procedural representational bridge
-> scripts encode temporal order, roles, normative expectations and context
-> scripts filter affordance salience
-> scripts can be internal, embodied and publicly scaffolded
-> skills can be understood as embodied clusters of scripts
-> explicit avowals can become implicit habits through implementation and repetition
-> disruption can make tacit scripts explicit and shift the salience landscape
-> norm change can therefore run in both explicit-to-implicit and implicit-to-explicit directions
```

## 4. Core source claims

### 4.1 Norms can shape perceived action possibilities

The paper's starting claim is stronger than a late-stage decision-bias account. Social norms can affect which interpersonal actions become perceptually salient or soliciting for a given agent, while other physically possible actions may fail to appear as live possibilities at all.

The authors therefore focus on interpersonal affordances: opportunities for interaction with another agent, understood in the ecological context of larger action sequences rather than as isolated object-action pairs.

### 4.2 Representation versus habit

The paper reconstructs a tension between a semantic / representational route and an ecological / habit / education-of-intention route.

Zipoli Caiani is used to motivate the claim that semantic information, including ownership and other norm-laden information, can modulate affordance selection and appears to require at least implicit representational content. Segundo-Ortin is used to motivate the alternative that socially guided participation can educate attention and intention without requiring explicit norm representations in the agent.

Berio and Musholt retain the importance of socially situated habit learning while arguing that explicit norm articulation can also reshape habits, attention and action possibilities, so a satisfactory account must explain traffic in both directions.

### 4.3 Scripts are norm-laden event-schemas

Scripts are context-sensitive structures for expected event sequences. Relevant properties include temporal ordering, role allocation, descriptive expectations, prescriptive / proscriptive force, automatic activation, implicit procedural knowledge, hierarchical nesting and flexible sub-scripts.

A script can therefore organize competent action in a familiar situation without the agent being able to state the governing social rule.

### 4.4 Scripts are role-indexed filters on affordance salience

Because scripts carry roles and normative expectations, the same physical object or person can afford different socially meaningful actions to differently positioned agents in the same broad setting.

Safe source compression:

```text
same physical possibility
+ different active script / social role
-> different socially salient invitation or prohibition
```

The paper does not claim that the affordance itself must therefore be representational.

### 4.5 Scripts can be socially distributed

The authors reject a narrowly intracranial reading of scripts. Scripts can be partly internalized information-bearing structures and partly public artifacts encoded in language, signage, dress codes, spatial layouts and recurrent social practices.

Thus:

```text
script possession
!= possession of one explicit internal rule
```

and can instead involve competent participation in socio-material scaffolding.

### 4.6 Skills as embodied scripts

Using Birch's norm-psychology discussion, the paper links normative cognition with practical skill. Technical norms provide criteria of better or worse performance; scripts extend such standards over time and social roles.

The authors suggest that a well-learned skill can be understood as an embodied script or cluster of scripts, while public teaching practices can make control routines socially transmissible.

### 4.7 Script compliance includes internal and external pressure

Internalizing a script can make deviation feel awkward or wrong. Social embedding can also expose deviation to blame or sanction. The paper uses this to explain why technical mistakes and social norm violations may share an affective structure of felt deviation from an internalized pattern.

This is a source-level phenomenological claim. It does not establish an identity between norm-violation discomfort and any SRT friction variable.

### 4.8 Power includes asymmetry in script interruption

The paper stresses that social actors can occupy unequal positions not only with respect to which actions appear possible, but also with respect to who can challenge, interrupt or sanction a running script.

Source-backed structure:

```text
role-indexed action space
+
role-indexed response / contestation space
```

The examples of gendered, racist, ableist and adultist interaction show how scripts may make actions available to some agents while making protest, interruption or correction less available or more costly to others.

### 4.9 Scripts are flexible rather than deterministic

Scripts need not prescribe a single next move. They can define a structured range of acceptable actions and responses, tolerate local variation, switch registers after mismatch, and contain nested sub-scripts adapted to context.

The source is therefore better read as describing a structured admissible region than a deterministic trajectory.

### 4.10 Explicit-to-implicit transition

Using Kelly's distinction between internalized and avowed norms, the paper argues that an abstract avowal must be translated into more specific application conditions / implementation intentions before repeated enactment can become habitual.

```text
explicit avowal
-> specific application rule
-> repeated enactment
-> habitual / implicit script
```

### 4.11 Implicit-to-explicit transition through disruption

Conversely, disrupting an expected interaction can bring a previously tacit script into attention. Once explicit, participants can reflect on the norms embedded within it and potentially modify the script.

Social contestation can therefore shift the salience landscape, opening some interpersonal affordances and eliminating others even before broad structural change occurs. The source does not show that every disruption produces durable social change.

## 5. Strongest SRT interfaces

### 5.1 Role-indexed invitation gating

Current SRT social cognition already distinguishes a broad affordance landscape from the subset that becomes a current invitation field. The source adds a disciplined social refinement:

```text
social role / position
+ active script
+ interaction history
-> which affordances become salient or soliciting
```

The increment is not merely that norms bias a final choice. Social history and role can participate in shaping which actions enter the effective candidate field in the first place.

### 5.2 Distributed script as a local carrier of historical constraint

The distributed-script view supports an SRT P3 question:

> Where is the effective historical constraint carried?

The carrier may be distributed across neural habit, body skill, language, spatial organization, public artifacts and other agents. This is compatible with a local social-cognition projection of `L_2` downward constraint but does not license:

```text
script = L_2
```

### 5.3 Supportive versus choice-replacing automation

The skill discussion blocks a simplistic equation:

```text
automation = less freedom
```

A well-learned low-level script can reduce routine selection burden and release capacity for higher-level novelty, revision and situated response. The SRT-relevant question is whether automation supports or replaces future live selectability at the relevant level.

### 5.4 Constraint reselection / `meta-selectability` must route into existing reselectability work

The paper's bidirectional norm-change architecture motivates a broad P3 diagnostic:

```text
implicit script
-> disruption / spotlighting
-> script becomes explicitly inspectable
-> possible revision
-> alternative enactment
-> possible new sedimentation
```

Working phrase:

> **`meta-selectability` = the capacity for constraints that shape what becomes selectable to become inspectable, contestable and revisable.**

This is **not** a second independent SRT mechanism and is not identical to the author-confirmed 2026-08-09 H3/I5 second-order-selection candidate. It is a social-cognition entry condition within the broader repository `reselectability` family.

The stricter SRT distinction is:

```text
constraint becomes revisable
!=
I5-qualified second-order selection
```

An I5-qualified candidate additionally requires the same historical bearing position's consequence return, comparison-scale / generation-rule rewrite, a changed future selectable space, and persistent later efficacy.

J5 then classifies the depth of reopening. A social revision may be R1-like (weight), R2-like (category / role boundary), R3-like (candidate-generation mechanism), or R4-like (concern boundary). `Reselect(Gamma_soc)` is therefore not automatically R3; **alternative-generation changes are specifically R3-like**.

### 5.5 Perceptual opening is not historical efficacy

The source can support a shift in perceived affordances without establishing that the shift survives resistance or changes future social structure.

```text
new possibility becomes visible
!=
new possibility becomes historically effective
```

Durable change requires enactment and writeback beyond momentary salience change.

## 6. Reverse correction / pressure on SRT

1. **Do not identify scripts with `L_2`.** Scripts can be local carriers / projections of historically stabilized social constraints, not the entire `L_2` history domain.
2. **Do not infer that all automation destroys freedom.** P1-T05 says script execution does not **by itself** constitute a Real Choice Moment; it does not imply that skill automation cannot support higher-level live choice.
3. **Do not infer Real Choice from disruption alone.** A new response, explicit awareness or option generation does not by itself satisfy stronger path-efficacy, consequence-bearing and history-efficacy requirements.
4. **Do not reduce norm-violation discomfort to `Psi_f`.** Awkwardness, blame, affective mismatch and felt error are not canonical `Psi_f` without independently specified bearing / payability / consequence-return structure.
5. **Do not reduce salience to `d-value`.** A norm can change salience without establishing stake-coupled concern or irreversible-risk sensitivity.
6. **Do not equate broad `meta-selectability` with I5 second-order selection.** The former can describe inspectability / contestability / revision capacity before same-bearer consequence return or comparison-scale rewrite is shown.
7. **Do not treat the paper as a neural-mechanism result.** The authors leave the precise low-level cognitive / visual mechanism open.
