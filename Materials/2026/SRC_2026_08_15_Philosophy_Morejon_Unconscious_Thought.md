# SourceCard: Gil Morejón, *The Unconscious of Thought in Leibniz, Spinoza, and Hume*

## 1. One-line summary

Morejón reconstructs an early-modern "unconscious of thought" in which conscious perception, desire, belief and reasoning depend on positive, structured processes that need not themselves be conscious: Leibnizian imperceptible perceptions, Spinozist inadequate ideas and obscure determinations of desire, and Humean habits that form and guide idea-to-idea transitions before reflective awareness.

## 3. Introduction — thought is not coextensive with consciousness

Morejón's central target is the philosophical tendency to identify thought with what is consciously accessible. The book argues instead for an **unconscious of thought** that is not merely accidentally unnoticed content. In the strongest cases, non-conscious elements are conditions for the possibility and determinate form of conscious thought itself.

The Introduction organizes the book around three cases:

```text
Leibniz -> unconscious perception
Spinoza -> inadequate ideas and unconscious determinations of desire
Hume    -> unconscious habit and synthetic passages of thought
```

The motivating problem is involuntarism: conscious judgment does not guarantee control over what one perceives, desires, believes or does. The book therefore separates conscious endorsement from the determining powers that shape actual thought and action.

**Text anchors:** Introduction, pp. 9-20; outline, pp. 24-28.

## 4. Leibniz — imperceptible perception and integration

### 4.1 Perception is not apperception

The Leibniz chapter rejects the identification of perception with conscious awareness. Petites perceptions can be real perceptions without being individually apperceived. They are not merely hidden completed conscious contents; they can be sub-threshold constituents of what later becomes conscious perception.

The sea-roar example supplies the strongest structure: a conscious perception of the roar depends on innumerable minute perceptions that are not individually conscious. Conscious perception is therefore treated as a singular integration of a multiplicity rather than as an atomic datum.

```text
imperceptible multiplicity
-> differential relations / composition
-> threshold of integration
-> determinate conscious perception
```

**Text anchors:** Chapter 1, especially "Imperceptible Perceptions," pp. 34-43; concluding synthesis, pp. 65-67.

### 4.2 Perspective as differential clarity / obscurity

For Leibniz, monads differ not because each contains an entirely different world but because the same world is expressed with different distributions of clarity and obscurity. Morejón uses this to emphasize the singularity of a perspective: what becomes foreground depends on a differentiated background that remains mostly obscure.

This also supports a continuity claim: absence of apperception does not imply absence of perception. Sleep, fainting or other lapses in conscious access therefore do not require the underlying perceptual process to cease.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 1, pp. 34-43.

### 4.3 Inclination, hidden non-equivalence, and freedom pressure

Leibniz rejects strict equilibrium indifference. Apparent equality between alternatives may conceal imperceptible inclinations. Conscious inability to state why one option is preferred therefore does not establish real equality of the underlying determinants.

Morejón uses this to pressure voluntarist freedom: finite agents cannot consciously exhaust the actual infinity of determinants involved in perception and inclination. He nevertheless reconstructs a Leibnizian residual freedom in terms of degrees of activity rather than an unconditioned faculty of arbitrary choice.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 1, "Inclination and Determination," pp. 43-55; "Infinitesimal Agency," pp. 65-67.

## 5. Spinoza — inadequacy, desire, and persistent determination

### 5.1 Inadequate does not simply mean false

Morejón stresses that Spinozist inadequacy is fundamentally **partiality**. An inadequate idea can contain positive determination while failing to express its causes adequately. Adequacy is therefore not merely accurate correspondence; it is tied to understanding causal genesis.

```text
true description
!=
adequate grasp of genesis
```

A finite mind necessarily possesses many inadequate ideas because it encounters effects without fully expressing all of the causes involved in their production.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 2, pp. 71-91.

### 5.2 Consciousness of effects is not transparent access to causes

The mind knows its body and external bodies through affections produced in encounters. What appears consciously is therefore an effect or mixture whose complete generative causes are not directly given. More reflection does not automatically solve this limitation: an idea of an inadequate idea does not thereby become a complete expression of the causal nexus that generated it.

This yields a recurrent distinction across the chapter:

```text
conscious access to an effect
!=
transparent access to its causes
```

**Text anchors:** Chapter 2, pp. 84-99.

### 5.3 The inadequate persists after correction

A central Spinozist result in Morejón's reading is that what is positive in an inadequate idea is not destroyed merely because a true or more adequate idea appears. The familiar sun-distance example shows that corrected astronomical knowledge removes an error without deleting the perceptual imagination itself.

```text
new true idea
!=
automatic deletion of prior positive determination
```

This is why epistemic correction and transformation of the determining structure must remain distinct.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 2, "The Persistence of the Inadequate and Spinozist Consciousness," pp. 99-105.

### 5.4 Desire is not exhausted by conscious wanting

Chapter 3 extends the argument from ideas to desire. Appetite or conatus remains operative whether or not it is consciously represented. Conscious desire is therefore only a partial expression of an already operative striving whose determinants include bodily affections, encounters, inadequate ideas, associative history and external relations.

Morejón describes desire as an effectively unknown vector along which a finite being's power is expressed. The explicit reason a person gives for an action need not exhaust the causal structure of the desire that moved the person.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 3, especially "The Unconscious of Desire," pp. 130-136.

### 5.5 Truth and determining power are different axes

Spinoza's affect theory supplies one of the book's strongest anti-voluntarist results: true knowledge does not restrain an affect merely in virtue of being true. A contrary configuration must itself acquire sufficient affective power to counter the prior determination.

This supports the source-level distinction:

```text
epistemic status of an idea
!=
its determining efficacy
```

The claim is narrower than any thesis that truth is generally fitness-inferior or behaviorally powerless.

**Text anchors:** Chapters 2-3, especially pp. 91-105 and 108-130.

## 6. Hume — belief, habit, and the synthetic movement of thought

### 6.1 Causal necessity is not given in singular experience

Morejón reconstructs Hume's critique of causal knowledge at three levels: empirical causal claims, the universal principle that whatever begins must have a cause, and the metacritical question of how the idea of necessary connection arises at all.

In a singular experience, the mind is given distinct perceptions and their succession or contiguity, not a directly perceived necessary connection. Matters of fact therefore cannot achieve demonstrative certainty in Hume's strict sense.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 4, "Secret Powers," pp. 147-161.

### 6.2 Belief differs from fiction by manner of conception

Hume's important distinction is not between special belief-content and fictional content. The same propositional or imagistic content can in principle be conceived as fiction or believed as fact. Belief is characterized by a different **manner of conception**: greater force, vivacity, solidity, firmness or steadiness, making the idea more influential and action-guiding.

```text
content(I)
!=
belief-weight(I)
```

**Text anchors:** Chapter 4, pp. 163-166.

### 6.3 Habit forms and executes below reflective awareness

Repeated conjunction is necessary for Humean causal belief because one isolated instance cannot distinguish accidental co-presence from a stable expectation. Repetition produces a habitual transition so that a present impression immediately carries the mind toward an expected idea without a new conscious inference.

Morejón distinguishes two unconscious dimensions:

```text
formation of habit -> largely insensible / involuntary
execution of habit -> transition can occur before reflection
```

The walking-toward-a-river example is decisive: the person does not consciously replay all past cases before stopping. Custom operates before reflection.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 4, "Belief, Habit, Synthesis," pp. 162-170.

### 6.4 Habit creates passageways rather than merely storing records

Morejón's strongest Humean formulation is that habit is a principle of synthesis. It does not merely preserve past contents; it creates pathways from one idea to another in advance of reflective consciousness. The book compares this to water carving channels into a landscape.

```text
past repetition
-> habitual passage
-> changed probability / felt inevitability of the next transition
```

The book also stresses that habit itself remains explanatorily obscure: Hume can describe its regular role while refusing a final rational derivation of why repetition produces this synthetic force.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 4, pp. 167-171.

### 6.5 There is no habit-free standpoint for correction

Hume's problem of correction is recursive. Bad generalization, prejudice and unreliable expectation arise from the same associative machinery that makes ordinary practical reasoning possible. Corrective general rules are themselves learned habits. Morejón therefore summarizes the Humean position as **there is no outside to habit**.

Correction requires contrary habits, broader experience and re-formed rules rather than a purely external rational tribunal that deletes history in one step.

**Text anchors:** Chapter 4, "Hume's Antinomy and the Problem of Correction," pp. 171-177.

## 7. Conclusion — obscurity and involvement

Morejón rejects two simple pictures of the unconscious:

```text
unconscious = conscious content merely hidden
```

and

```text
unconscious = a totally unrelated second domain
```

He proposes **involvement** to name the determinate way non-conscious processes participate in and condition conscious thought, and **obscurity** to mark that these conditions are not thereby rendered clearly and distinctly accessible to consciousness.

```text
conscious mode of thought
<- obscure involvement of non-conscious conditions
```

The conclusion explicitly reconnects the three cases:

- Leibniz: imperceptible perceptions are involved in conscious perception;
- Spinoza: inadequate ideas, bodily encounters and obscure causes are involved in affect and desire;
- Hume: habits of association and expectation are involved in conscious passages of thought.

The book's final claim is not that these mechanisms are identical. Their concrete configuration is singular to each mind, and formal description does not exhaust that concrete singularity.

**Text anchors:** Conclusion, pp. 178-184.

## 8. Limits

1. The book is a historical-philosophical construction, not an empirical theory of unconscious cognition.
2. Morejón's synthetic reading is influenced by Deleuze and a broader anti-voluntarist genealogy; source-level claims should not be attributed directly to Leibniz, Spinoza or Hume when they are Morejón's reconstruction.
3. Leibnizian pre-established harmony, Spinozist necessitarianism, and Humean skepticism about causal knowledge are distinct metaphysical commitments; the book does not collapse them into one doctrine.
4. The source does not define `L0/L1/L2`, `d`, `Psi_f`, `T_dir`, `kappa`, Stable ISP, Real Choice Moment, embodied position, or SRT stake.
5. The source does not establish that consciousness is merely habit, that all unconscious structure is `L_2`, or that introspective opacity proves ontological indeterminacy.
6. Humean force/vivacity is not an empirical scalar supplied by the monograph and should not be identified with SRT quantities.
